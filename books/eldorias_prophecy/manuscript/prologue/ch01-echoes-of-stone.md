# Ch. 1 — Echoes of Stone
<!-- Track: 1. Echoes of Stone | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, early winter -->

The tower kept the cold the way old men kept grudges. Eldrin Nightshade had fed this room a fire every winter for thirty years, and every spring it gave the warmth back as a rumor. He no longer minded. He was a man accustomed to things that did not fully answer when you called them.

Snow had fallen on Thaloria since dusk. He knew it without looking — the candle drawing its flame into a spear against the draft, the stone of the walls letting out its small, specific crack as it cooled. Outside, under that whisper of a roof, the scholar-city lay in its celebrated quiet: the long dark of the archives, the unlighted windows of the college, the bell tower whose hour had not struck correctly in his lifetime. They called this the Silent Century, and they meant it kindly. A hundred years of peace, stitched over a wound.

Eldrin did not trust seams. He had spent his life studying how things healed, and the first thing any true healer learned was that a wound does not heal. It is replaced. He had written that sentence forty years ago in the margin of his thesis, and he had never once found cause to strike it out.

His desk was a spine of stone. Cores, drilled from the deep places of Eldoria in his youth, when he had still believed the world's bones would confess to him if he only asked properly, laid end to end and polished until the bedding planes shone. Beside them, the journals: thirty years of his own cramped hand, the margins gnawed with questions. He knew every page by the shape of its doubt.

He took up the newest core and turned it to the lamp. It had come from the quarries under Lake Aethera, one hundred and forty feet down, and it was a faithful liar, like all of them. The top: six inches of honest, patient accumulation — root scars, a wedge of clay where a flood had dug. Below that, the long banded quiet of the Silent Century, generation after generation of sediment pressed to slate, orderly as prayer. And below that, the seam where the world stopped.

He had given it a name years ago, in a paper the college declined to print. The Echoes. Bands in the deep stone where the record simply ended — not blurred, not eroded, but clean, as if the world's hand had been lifted from the page and set down again a line lower. Layer, restart, layer, restart. When he aligned the cores, the breaks lined up across all of them like ruled ink. A neatness no flood, no landslide, no slow drowning of the land had ever produced.

The geologists called it a discontinuity. The college preferred *compression*. Eldrin's own marginal note, in the violet ink he used only for heresy, read: *What cuts a world clean through at the same depth, three times, in the same century?*

Nothing cuts stone, he had been told, gently and repeatedly. Stone was the one honest record. Stone did not lie and did not forget.

And yet the stone kept insisting the world had ended before. More than once. And each time it had ended, something had set it down again — not healed, just placed — and the record had resumed as though the interruption had never been.

He set the core back in its slot and let his hand rest on the runes he had incised along its length: a spiral of tiny geometric figures, patient as a tide. Scholar-magic. He had not cast in a month. It was not that he lacked the strength — the strength was a habit, like keeping a sword oiled — but that magic was a debt, and it drew things. Light a lamp in a dark water and the water comes to look. The Aether answered every command with attention, and attention, in this thin, tired age, was not free. He had learned that lesson in a tower that smelled of burnt quartz, and he had never forgotten it. The masters taught that a mage's soul was a voice and the world was a song. Eldrin had learned that the song was listening, and what listened was not always friendly.

In the songs the children sang, the Great Darkness was a bogey — a winter that lasted three thousand years, a sky that went out, a god that swallowed the moon and got indigestion. The bards called it *stories half-told*, and meant it kindly, meaning no one remembered the whole of it. Eldrin thought the bards had it precisely backwards. The Great Darkness was not half-remembered. It had been told — cleanly, completely, once, in a voice that left nothing to chance — and the telling had grown over the wound like a scar, and the scar was what they called history.

He had gone looking, once, for the place the truth should have kept. The Forbidden Archive was not forbidden in the way the name suggested. It was forbidden the way a locked door is forbidden when the key has been lost and no one wishes to admit it. Beneath the college, in the iron rooms where the oldest texts were kept against the damp and the dark and the occasional philosopher, there was a shelf. He had found it empty. Not raided — a raided shelf leaves dust, and dust is a record of its own. This shelf had been *cleaned*. Someone had stood exactly where he stood and taken everything, and taken it carefully, and left the shelf to be repainted and re-lettered with the wrong date.

That was the Gap. Not a hole in the record. A place where the record had been tidied.

Every chronicle that touched the year 0 GD ended mid-sentence or resumed in a different hand. Every eyewitness account that survived contradicted the next by precisely the details that mattered — the color of the sky, the direction of the wind, the number of days the dark had lasted. The cataloguers had spent careers reconciling them and had quietly gone mad, or quietly gone quiet, which for a scholar was the worse fate. Eldrin had spent his own career proving the contradictions were not accidents. Accident did not repeat. Error did not correct itself in the same place, generation after generation, with the same tidy violence.

The Gap was not an absence. It was a scar. And scars, as he was fond of telling the college, do not fade. They wait.

He was alone in believing it. That was the true weight of the thing — not that he was right, but that he was right alone, and the being right was not worth the being alone. The college pitied him the way it pitied its lame cats. Master Eldrin, the scholars said over their wine, chasing the year that never was. Let him have his strata and his theories. He is harmless, and the archives are the better for his dusting.

He let them. It cost him less than arguing.

And then, this season, the world had begun to change, and he had begun to keep a second journal.

He could not say when it started. Months, perhaps; a season's turn. It lived in the small wrong textures of the city: wine turning thin in the same cellars every year; cats refusing the same thresholds, morning after morning; the silence developing a grain, like glass about to give. He was a scholar — he demanded of himself that every observation carry a date — and so he had dated the wrongness, entering it in the plainest of his books, and the wrongness had kept coming, and the dates had marched past his ability to call them coincidence.

It felt, he wrote, in a rare moment of want of precision, *like a crack in glass you cannot find: you hear the note it will sing when it breaks, and you know it is only a question of when.*

The Aether was thin. Thinner than his oldest colleagues could remember, thinner than the oldest chronicles in the Archive — the ones that did not contradict themselves — would account for. The world was holding its breath. The Silent Century had built its peace on top of that held breath and called the held breath calm.

Then the letter had come. Ten days old, from the monastery at Silverrun, in the cramped, kind hand of a brother he had corresponded with for half his life. Eldrin had read it twice, which he did only for letters that were trying to say something else. Scholars passed through Thaloria constantly; the city was a terminus for footnotes. But these travelers, the brother wrote, had asked a strange question, over and over, in every village and shrine and inn along the river: not about the archives, not about the strata, not about the old gods. They asked about the Anchor. What it was. Where it lay. Who kept it.

The brother had laughed it off as the fashion of eccentrics. Eldrin did not laugh. He did not know the word any better than the brother did — it was in no record, no song, no corner of the Gap. But he knew, with the same animal certainty that tells a lamb the ground is wrong, that men who asked one careful question in every village were not scholars. Scholars quarreled; scholars kept their questions in their sleeves and drew them out one at a time, like cards they were not sure they wanted to play. These men had moved in a line, asking one question, never resting, never quarreling, never explaining.

Something was searching. And it was searching quietly, and quiet searching meant it did not want to be found, and what did not want to be found was not here to ask permission.

He had set the letter aside. He was a scholar; he dealt in what could be proven, and the thinning Aether could not be proven, and the travelers could not be proven to matter, and the clean shelf in the Archive could not be proven to be anything but an old man's obsession wearing a scholar's robes. He had set the letter aside, and then he had come back to it, and then he had come back to it again, and that, more than the letter itself, was what frightened him. His life was built of small rooms of proof, and one of them had a draft under the door. He could not find the crack, and the crack could not find him, and the whole city slept on above the buried seam like a town that does not feel the ground give way beneath the weight of its own bells.

The candle was nearly down. He reached to trim the wick.

It went out.

No — it did not go out. It flattened. The flame drew itself into a needle, then into a line, then into nothing, and the room did not grow dark. It grew *wrong*. The cold came up off the floor like a question. The strata cores stood in their slots like a spine laid bare, and the violet ink on the map of the world began to glow — faint, then not faint — and the glow ran up the wall like water climbing a thread. The journals trembled on their shelves, not shaking, just present, as if every one of them had something it had been waiting thirty years to say.

Eldrin's hand was still extended toward the dead candle. He heard the bell — the old bell, the one that had not struck correctly in his lifetime — begin to sound, all its hours at once, wet and far away and wrong, as if it were ringing underwater. Or under time.

Then the room dissolved.

It did not fall apart. It went soft at the edges, the way a scene goes soft at the edge of a dream, and through the softening he saw, not the walls of the tower, not the stone of Thaloria, not the seams of the world he had spent his life trying to read — a sky.

A violet sky. The color of a bruise and a wound at once, pouring light that was not light across a world that was not his.

And he heard, or felt, or tasted — for it came through no organ a shelf could name — a word. A word in no language he knew, shaped like a storm, wearing a name.

*Vorgos.* The Stormbringer. The voice that spoke through the records that contradicted themselves, the wind that moved through the empty shelves, the one thing in the Gap that was not missing.

And the voice said: *Eldrin.*

The bell went on ringing. The sky went on burning. And Eldrin Nightshade, who had believed himself alone in a hundred years of wrongness, understood with a certainty that had nothing to do with proof that he was not alone at all.

Someone had been listening.
