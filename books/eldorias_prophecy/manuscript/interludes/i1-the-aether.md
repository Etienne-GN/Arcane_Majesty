# Interlude — The Aether
<!-- POV: Vorgos | Date: 501 AGD, late winter -->

He had no body to stand in, and he stood anyway. The Aether had no floor and no ceiling and no horizon, and he had never needed them. What it had was the between — the long, living tension where the three frequencies held their distance, and where, for longer than any language had kept a record, he had kept the books.

The books were not books. He read resonance the way the scholars of Thaloria read vellum — by the settled habits of a reader who has outlived every text and learned to read the paper itself. The Aether was not empty. It was the attention between the stations, the silence in which every song was held. It listened, and he was what its listening had become.

The three planes lay open around him like accounts, three columns of light without color, because he was past matter. Auroria above, high and gilded, paying interest without ever being spent. Eldoria between, the crowded column — births and burnings and the slow arithmetic of a world that spent without keeping count. And Nythoria below, the dark column, the one that compounded, that never paid a thing but grew.

He read them for the balance, and the balance, this season, was reading itself back to him with a sound like a blade drawn and not yet used.

At the center of the Aether, where the between was thinnest, the Great Rift burned. He had torn it, and did not trouble himself with the difference between owning a wound and being one; the Rift was his the way a scar is yours — entered on your own account, never written off. Five centuries it had stood open, a raw bleed where all three frequencies crashed into one another. He did not regret the Rift. That was not the same as being easy with it. He had spent a debt he was not owed to buy the world its silence, and the silence was now coming due — and when a debt comes due, the books are the first place the trouble shows.

He turned his attention below the Rift, where the dark column thickened into a pit, and he read the Lock.

The Lock had a name. That was the cruelty of it, and he had known it when he made it, because names were what you gave to things you meant to hold onto. Kael. The Star-Guard who had been made into a door. The resonance was the hinge, and the hinge was wearing.

He read the erosion the way a clerk reads a column past its term. The Lock had been entered at the turn of the age — the heir of a stolen light, a spark in his blood he had never been meant to carry — and he had carried it two centuries since, not as the books had predicted. They had predicted a slow spend, a debt drawn down across a thousand years; what was happening instead was decay: the resonance eroding the Star-Guard from within, eating the man to feed the door he had become. The rate of the eating was ahead of schedule — months ahead, then a season. He had not predicted it; surprise was a luxury he had spent five centuries learning to do without.

He noted the entry and moved on to the worse one.

The Generals had caught the scent. Three signatures, writing themselves across the Aether for weeks, three columns of wrongness converging on the same quiet coordinate — the ruins where the Anchor lay in its long keeping. Each was certain the stone would open the way for its master, and each was certain the other two were wrong. Malphas, who whispered — doubt carried like silt, working the seams of the world. Xarathos, who burned — a fallen light, moving with the fixed hunger of something told to burn. And Voraun, the quiet one, whose signature was a subtraction, a place where the Aether simply was not, as if a man had been cut from the page with a careful knife — and he had been quiet for months, and quiet that long meant close.

He had moved Eldrin early because of it. The Lock's erosion was a light, and light draws things — the same law that made a lit ward a beacon in Eldoria made a failing lock a beacon in the between. The dark had not needed to be told where to look; it had only needed the door to weaken. So he had reached through the Aether, called the one man who was awake, and pointed him east.

The third reading came last, saved like an overdue entry.

Eldrin.

Not the strongest. Vorgos had moved pieces across five centuries, and he valued a piece in proportion to what it could be made to do without noticing it had been moved. The strongest had a sense of their own weight; they pushed back, asked questions, wanted to understand the game first. The prophecy had had its chosen ones once — a line of them, groomed and failed and buried — and he had stopped using the chosen, because the chosen kept arriving with designs of their own, and a piece with designs is a piece that will break the board.

Eldrin was not the strongest. He was not the chosen. He was the available — the piece that had sat on the board for sixty years, quiet, exact, asking no one's permission, only that the record be true. He was the one awake, and that was his whole qualification, and it was enough: of all the pieces in that thin, tired age, he was the one who had noticed that the world had been reset before, and that noticing was the only strength that could not be moved.

A pawn upon a cosmic board.

He did not think it with contempt. There was no contempt in Vorgos; contempt is a tax a stronger thing levies on a weaker one, and he had outlived the ledger's patience for taxes. But he had moved pawns before, and he had seen what a pawn became when it was walked to the far side of the board. Anya, plucked from Auroria at the founding of the age — a scholar of the light, taken from a library and dropped into the pit where the dark had come awake. Not the strongest, not the chosen — the one in reach — and she had walked the length of the board and become the thing that held. Kael, at the turn of the age, the heir of a stolen spark, made not into a soldier but into a door; the vessel, the only one who could carry the light without being consumed, and he was carrying it still, two hundred years of it, the erosion eating him from inside like interest. And now Eldrin — the third in the line of pieces that were not chosen, only used, and used well, because none of them had known they were being used until it was too late.

He was not cruel. He had settled that account long ago: cruelty was waste. He had made no one suffer for the pleasure of it — he had made them suffer because the ledger required it, and the difference mattered to him, and he knew it did not matter to them, and he held both facts without trouble.

And yet. He could feel it. That was the one entry he did not write down, because the books were for what was owed, not for what was felt. The monument he had made of Kael, the door he had made of a soldier's body, the vigil he had made of a woman's grief, and now the long road he had made of an old man's last honest years. He had made a monument of a man. More than one. None of them had asked for it, none would thank him, and he did not need them to — he needed them to hold. That was the only need he had ever admitted.

The almost-regret came. It always came at this point, when the pieces were in motion and there was nothing left to calculate — a thin, high note in the resonance, like the note a bell gives before it has decided whether it is a note or a crack. It was not for Eldrin, not for Kael, not for Anya, though all three had earned it. It was for the shape of the game itself: that a world could only be held by being spent, that every age was a debt and every holder a borrower and every borrower, in the end, became the interest.

He set it aside. That was the whole of his discipline, and it had survived five centuries of them — not the denial, which was a lie, but the setting-aside, which was a fact. The ledger did not close for pity. The columns did not balance for grief. The Rift burned, the Lock was still eroding, the three signatures were still converging on the ruins, and somewhere east of Thaloria, under a sky the color of a held breath, the piece he had moved was still walking.

He had not made him the strongest. He had not made him the chosen. He had made him the one who was awake and pointed him at the only thing that could hold — and the pointing was the tragedy, and the tragedy was the plan, and the plan was all that stood between the three columns and the pit.

The schedule was in arrears. The race was real. And Eldrin Nightshade was the piece he had moved.

He did not weep; he had no eyes to weep with, and it would not have helped if he had. He returned to the books, as he had for five centuries, and would until the columns balanced or the world did not. The Rift burned. The Aether listened. And somewhere in the thin, late-winter silence between the frequencies, a man walked east.
