# Interlude — The Lock
<!-- POV: Kael + Anya | Date: 501 AGD, midsummer -->

The sound lived in the stone. It was not loud. It was low — a scream, held, tuned to the pitch of a thing pressed past its patience, the note a rope gives when it is overstrained. Her masters in Auroria had called it resonance, as if a hand might tune it. It was not music. It had been under the floor of the Sanctuary when she was thrown into the pit, and it had been under the floor through every age since. She had stopped listening to it a hundred years ago. She had stopped being able not to listen to it some time after that.

The Sanctuary was a single room, and the room was the floor — a pallet, a basin, a hearth that had not burned in living memory, because the only light in the room was Kael. The stolen spark ran in his blood and showed at the seams of him, a thin gold through the skin of his wrists. It was the whole lamp of the place, and it was killing him.

The prison lay at the heart of the floor. The Sanctuary was a room built around a wound, and the wound was the prison — a place where the floor of the world had been torn once, at the founding, and sewn shut since, held closed by the resonance and by the man who carried it. Anya stood at its edge as she had stood most mornings for five centuries, and she read it the way she had once read books. The seal was not smooth, but its faults had been the faults of age, worn even by time in a straight line. Not the even wear, though. The uneven. The place where the note frayed.

It was fraying. She would have to tell him. She would not tell him until she had to.

He was behind her. She knew the sound of him the way she knew the sound of the floor — the drag of a man two hundred years into a weight that is never lifted. He came up to her shoulder, and the note did not change, and she did not say what she had seen, and he did not ask. That was the grammar of their mornings.

"Still holding," he said. It was not a question. It was the thing he said in place of good morning.

She turned, and the anger came up in her the way the note came up through the stone — constant, tuned, hers. He wore the erosion the way a soldier wears a wound he has stopped reporting. Two centuries of it showed in ways it had not a hundred years ago: the light nearer the surface than it had any right to be, new places where the flesh had thinned over the bone. The lamp showing through the paper. She had never been able to unsay it.

"You're thinner," she said. "Since the last moon."

"The light," Kael said. He flexed his hands, and the glow under the skin shifted, obedient. "The lock feeds on it. It always has."

"It feeds on you."

"It eats the man to feed the door." He said it without bitterness, the way a man recites a thing he has made his peace with, and that was the worst of it. He was not brave about it; this was not a choice but a price, paid without comment. He had been a Star-Guard when the spark was put into him, the heir of a stolen light he had never been meant to carry. When the prison began to fail he had been made into the door, the only vessel that could carry the light without being consumed — and the cruel arithmetic was that he was consumed anyway, just slowly, by the holding instead of the carrying. The light would be the death of him, a spoonful at a time.

She wanted to shake the stoicism out of him like a cork, and never had, because the anger was not against him and that was why it had nowhere to go. It was the shape of her love — a love with nowhere to put itself down, because the thing she loved was not a man to hold but a door that would not stop standing. Two centuries of watching him be eaten, and all she could offer the hunger was the same vigil she had offered the pit: herself, staying, refusing to look away.

Kael read her the way he read the resonance — by its changes, not its level. "You found a fray," he said. It was not a question.

"A fault in the seam, near the western face. It wasn't there last season."

"How small?"

"Finger's width." She did not look away; a man being eaten deserved the correct inventory. "It's violet, Kael. The stone isn't grey there anymore. It isn't a crack the stone made by itself. It's a crack someone is making from the other side."

She stopped there, and Kael waited, because he knew she would give him the whole of it or nothing, and she gave him the whole of it.

"Vorgos spoke. Last season, when the note began to fray. Through the note itself, not through the pit. He said a name."

Kael's hands went still. She had seen him take blows for two centuries without flinching, and the name made him flinch, or near enough — a pause, the way the note pauses before picking itself up again.

"Eldrin," she said. "A stranger. A scholar from the surface, an old man from some city of archives, walking east because Vorgos pointed him there. If he reaches the thing that holds, the door will hold again. That is the hope we have been given, Kael. A name, and a stranger's walking."

"Then he will," Kael said.

The absurdity of it rose in her, and she let it, because it was true. Five centuries of vigil. Two centuries of a man being eaten alive to keep a door shut. The three of them — Anya, Kael, and the dying note — were the whole of the world's defense, and the hope they had been handed was a name, delivered like a coin into a well. Every hope that had ever been lowered into this pit had been absurd, and every one of them had been all there was.

"That's not hope," she said. "That's his bookkeeping."

"It's a soldier's kind of hope." Kael looked at the seam, and the violet in it, and the light moved in his wrists like a thing turning in sleep. "A gate doesn't hold because the man holding it believes it will. It holds because if he doesn't, nothing else does. Vorgos has lied to every piece he has ever moved — he lied to me, he lied to you, pulling you out of a library and calling it a season's work — but he has never once told a piece the wrong name. He does not whisper a name he does not mean to keep."

"You've never met him. You don't know he's real."

"I know I'm real," Kael said. "And I know the spark that's killing me was real before I was. The light comes from someone who carried it first. If the carrying is real, then whoever took it up next is walking east right now, and my only job is to be holding the door when he arrives."

The resonance lived in him the way a heartbeat lives in a man — from the inside, in the walls of his chest. It had been that way so long he could not remember the before of it. He did not feel the eating as pain; pain was a thing that came and went, and this simply was, as constant as the floor. What he felt was the shape of the door he had become — the man-thickness worn down until barely enough of Kael remained to keep the light in. And that was the hour he hoped in, the thin hour, when holding was nearly all that was left of him: because the thinner he was, the closer the light sat to the surface, and the sooner someone else would have to carry it. It was a soldier's hope, and the only kind he knew how to keep.

"All that's left to us," he said, "is to hold the door until he sets it down. And if he doesn't — then we'll have held it. And we'll have held it long. And that will be the account."

She should have hated the sentence. She could not; it was the same sentence she had been living for five centuries, in the grey hours when the note under the floor was the only other voice in the world. You hold because holding is the work. The work does not end when the world does. And here he was, saying it back to her as if it were a hope instead of an exhaustion.

She turned away, back to the seam, because she could not let him see that the sentence had moved her — that the anger had, for a moment, gone quiet, the way the note goes quiet before a shift.

The shift came.

"Kael."

He was beside her in a stride; he felt it through the resonance before he saw it, in his own blood first, the way she felt it in her bones. The fissure had grown. Not much — a finger's width more, the grey stone curling back from the seal like a lip. But the violet that poured through it was brighter than at dawn, and it was not light, and it had a motion, a slow seeping that did not belong to stone, and beneath the scream — beneath the world's long note of holding — there was a second sound. Thin. Almost nothing. A note a blade gives when it is drawn and not yet used, coming from the wrong side of the door.

They stood at the edge of the seam, the two of them, and the fissure poured its small violet wrongness into the Sanctuary, and neither of them said the words. The words lived in the stone now, the way the note lived in it. The note was failing. The failure had a color and a direction. And somewhere far above them, in a country she would never see, a stranger walked, and the whole of the world's defense was a door that was holding, a woman who would not look away, and the last of a stolen light.

The fissure went on glowing. Small. Wrong. Violet.

Neither of them looked away.
