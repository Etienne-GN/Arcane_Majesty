# Ch. 14 — Inferno's Trial
<!-- Track: 8. Inferno's Trial | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, early summer -->

The ice gave way to burning heat, and the magma flowed beneath his feet.

That was the first country of the labyrinth, and it was a country in earnest — not a cave, not a tunnel, but a world, a vast hollowed continent under the mountain's heart, and the world was made of fire. The black stairs gave way to galleries of red and black stone, and the galleries opened into chambers as large as the halls of the old court, and through the chambers ran the rivers — not the rivers of the green valley, the ancient magic rivers of the surface, but rivers of the earth's own blood, slow and heavy and bright, pouring their light up over the stone. The heat was not the dry warmth of the Fire Gate's mouth. It was a presence, a pressure, a thing that stood on the chest and leaned. It came up through the boots and down through the crown of the head and in through the seams of the eyes, and it did not ask permission.

Eldrin walked into it the way a man walks into weather — with his head down, his breath measured, his attention on the ground before his feet. The wound in his side had settled into a low animal ache, the ache of a thing that had been told to wait. The broken staff was a crutch under his arm, the runes dead along the split, and he had wrapped the break with a strip of his coat until it was a length of scarred wood with a handhold, and it served. The needle turned. The heat leaned. And the walls of the labyrinth were written with the same hunger he had read at the gate — the cruder hand, the hungry hand, the runes of a thing that had carved them to feed — and the runes ran ahead of him into the red dark like a sentence that had been waiting for him to arrive.

And then the gate spoke his name.

He heard it first as a shape in the runes — a wrongness in the carved letters, a place where the hunger had organized itself into an order, a message. He stopped, and he read it the way he had read the strata for forty years, by the settled habits of a man who has learned to read the record beneath the writing. The runes on the wall before him were not a warding. They were a *summons*. They were a trap that had been built to recognize him, keyed to his name, seeded into the very stone of the labyrinth by a hand that had known he was coming.

The wall of fire rose out of the ground before him, and it spoke.

"Eldrin."

The voice was the fire's voice, and it was the voice of the hungry hand, and it was not a voice that threatened. It was a voice that *called* — the way a gate calls a traveler, the way a seal calls the one who was meant to break it. The fire ran up the wall before him, a sheet of living flame taller than the tower of his study, and through the flame, in the shape of the burning, he read his own name, written in the hungry hand, spelled out in the fall of the fire, and he understood that the Legion had not built a gauntlet against him.

They had built a *welcome*.

"A wall of fire, a gate of flame," the fire said, "that calls me by my given name." The words came out of the burning in a voice that was almost kind. "You have walked far, keeper. You have lost much. You have carried the needle through the storm and the doubt and the betrayal, and you have come at last to the door that was made for you. Do you not wonder what waits behind it?"

Eldrin stood at the foot of the wall of fire with the heat leaning on him and the runes spelling his name in the flame, and the wonder — the scholar's wonder, the endless question that had carried him out of the tower — rose in him like a tide. It rose, and he looked at it, and he did not feed it.

"I know what waits behind it," he said. "A stone. A door. A vigil. And a man who has been waiting for me to carry it out for him." He looked into the fire, into the shape of his own name. "You built this gate for me because you need me alive. You've built the whole road that way — the whisperer could have taken me at the summit, and he held his hand; the man I trusted could have cut my throat a hundred times, and he waited for the door. I know the shape of your hunger now. It's the only honest thing any of you have shown me."

The fire was quiet. The runes burned. And somewhere behind the wall, in the red heart of the labyrinth, the hunger that had carved the runes heard him, and the hearing was a retuning, the same retuning he had heard at the summit when he had refused the whisperer's vision.

"You have learned," the fire said, at last. "Good. A trial that cannot be grieved is a trial that cannot be taught. But learning is not passing, keeper. And the door is not the wall."

The wall did not part. The fire did not die. The gate of flame stood across the gallery, spelling his name, and the way through it was not a way a man could walk.

Eldrin opened the sight.

It came up out of him the way it had come up in the pass, only steadier now, trained, the second ledger the hermit had taught him to keep. And the world of the labyrinth resolved itself into two hands. The first hand was the visible world — the fire, the runes, the red stone, the heat. The second hand was the writing beneath the writing: the threads of the Aether running through the stone like rivers, the life of the place, the current that fed the fire and the hunger and the spell that spelled his name. And in the second hand, the wall of fire was not a wall.

It was a *signature*.

The fire was not meant to burn him. It was meant to *recognize* him — to read him the way the stone would read him, to test whether the hand that reached for the door was the hand the door was meant for. The runes that spelled his name were not a cage; they were a lock, and the lock was waiting for its key, and the key was not a word and not a gesture and not a thing a man could carry.

The key was the name itself. The fire was calling him by his given name, and the calling was the trial, and the trial was not a test of whether he could answer.

It was a test of whether he would answer as himself.

He walked into the fire.

He did not close the sight, and he did not raise a ward, and he did not draw a rune. He walked into the wall of flame with the needle turning in his skull and the wound in his side and the broken staff under his arm, and he did it the way the hermit had told him to walk past the Balrog — the only way past a thing that cannot be beaten, which is to not be its enemy. He walked into the fire as the man who had been summoned, as the keeper the door was waiting for, as Eldrin — not Eldrin the scholar, not Eldrin the pawn, not Eldrin the trusting friend. Just the name, spoken true, answered true.

And the fire knew him.

It did not burn him. It could not have — it had been built to recognize him, and recognition is not consumption. The heat passed through him the way the wind passes through a field, and the runes of his name went out along the wall like a line of candles, and the gate of flame opened, and beyond it the labyrinth's true country began: the galleries of the heart, the chambers of the deep, the place where the runes had been carved by a hand that had come to feed.

He walked through the opening and did not look back. The fire closed behind him, and the heat went with him, and the needle turned, and the way ran on.

The golems were waiting in the first of the great chambers.

He saw them by the light of the rivers — three of them, standing at the far side of a black gallery, and they were the size of the towers of the old court, and they were made of obsidian, of the black glass of the gate's mouth grown up into figures of terrible patience. They did not move. That was the first thing he noted, the scholar's note, filed before the fear could file it: they did not move, and they had not moved for a long time, and they were not asleep. They were *waiting*, and the waiting was the whole of their nature, the way the Balrog's keeping was the whole of its nature. They stood between him and the way on, and on their chests, cut into the black glass, were runes.

His runes.

He knew them before he understood them — the geometric figures of his own trade, the lock and the knot and the stopper, the figures the masters had taught him in his youth, cut into the obsidian chests of the Legion's golems in the exact hand he had been taught to draw them. The Legion had not seeded the labyrinth with his identity in the way of a threat. It had seeded it with his *language*. The golems would not fall to force, and they would not fall to fear, and they would not fall to any key but the one the carver had been taught to use.

The rune-frequencies. The sight showed him the threads of them — the current running through the obsidian, the note each golem was tuned to, the precise pitch of the runes that had been carved into their chests. Each golem was a bell, and each bell was waiting for its note, and the note was a figure of his own language, drawn at the exact frequency the obsidian had been cut to hold.

He drew. The chalk would not hold on the hot glass, and the vitriol would not run true in the heat, and so he drew the way he had drawn in the pass, with the thing that was left — with the soul, with the Aether, with the last of the winter's strength. He drew the first golem's rune in the air before it, in blue light, in violet at the edges, and he fed it, and the feeding was a wrenching — the Snap's cousin, the bill for a magic that was being spent in a place where the Aether was thin and hungry — and the blue figure crossed the gallery and struck the obsidian chest where the matching figure was cut.

The golem *answered*.

Not with violence. With resonance — the black glass sang, a deep stone note, and the note went through the chamber the way the broken man's note had gone through the vision, and the golem's waiting, which had been the whole of its nature, changed. It stepped aside. It did not bow, and it did not crumble, and it did not serve; it moved, one great slow stride, and the way opened past it.

He drew the second. The second was tuned a quarter-tone off, and the sight showed him the mismatch before the draw, and he adjusted, and the note sang true, and the second golem stepped aside. The third was the hardest — it was tuned to a figure he had not drawn since his youth, a figure the masters had called the *foundation*, the first figure in the language, the one every scholar cut into his first staff — and he had not drawn it in forty years, and he drew it now, and the drawing was the most expensive thing he had done since the pass, and the blue light of it went thin and nearly failed at the crossing, and he fed it the last of the winter, and it held, and the third golem stepped aside.

He stood in the black gallery with the three golems behind him and the Snap washing over him like a tide, and he had nothing left. That was the truth of it — he had spent the last of the winter's strength on the runes, and the world had gone grey, and his hands had a distance to them, and the way on ran ahead of him into the deep red dark.

And the deep red dark was not empty.

The Magma-Eaters came first — not from ahead, from *within*, from the rivers themselves, and they were the rivers' hunger made brief: shapes of molten light the size of hunting hounds, flowing up out of the flow and standing, bright and wrong, drawn by the blue light he had spent like a lamp draws the dark. They did not attack. They *reached* — they reached with the heat, with the hunger, with the attention of a thing that has been fed on the light of every fool who has ever carried magic into this country, and the reaching was the attack, and the attack was a drinking. He felt the last of his strength going out of him the way the ward's light had gone out in the first night, drawn up into the shapes of molten light like water into sand.

And the Cinder-Souls came behind them.

He had read of them, once, in the Archive's bestiary — a single honest page, the page of a hunter or a priest who had not survived the writing: *the burned, the ones the fire took before their time. They do not hate. They hunger for warmth, for life, for the breath that was taken from them. They will crowd around a living man the way the cold crowd around a fire, and they will not leave, and they will not be driven, and the fire they were taken by is the only thing they answer to.* He saw them now — shapes of ash and red light, hundreds of them, rising out of the stone where the fire had fed, reaching toward him with the particular reaching of the dead, and the reaching was not malice. It was the oldest hunger there is, the hunger of a thing that has lost its warmth and wants it back.

He was empty. He was grey. He had nothing left to spend — no soul, no light, no runes, no magic, nothing but the broken staff and the wound in his side and the needle in his skull and the understanding, at the bottom of the labyrinth, of what the hermit had meant when he said that the heat would burn the doubt out of him.

He had been wrong about the trial. He had been wrong all along, the way he had been wrong at the summit and wrong in the winter and wrong at the landing. He had thought the labyrinth was a test of his magic, of his craft, of his learning — a gauntlet of runes and frequencies and the scholar's exactness. But the golems had not tested his magic. They had tested his *language* — whether the man who had been carved into the runes was the man who would come. And the Magma-Eaters and the Cinder-Souls were not testing his strength at all.

They were testing his emptiness.

The fire burned. The hunger reached. And Eldrin Nightshade, empty and grey and spent at the bottom of the mountain's heart, with the Cinder-Souls crowding around him like the cold around a dying fire, did the only thing he had left.

He refused to fall.

He did not fight. He had nothing to fight with. He did not run — there was nowhere to run that the heat did not own. He stood in the red dark with the shapes of the burned reaching for him and the shapes of the molten light drinking the last of his light, and he stood *still*, and the standing still was not surrender and it was not prayer and it was not the last of his strength. It was the whole of it — the refusal, the stubbornness, the stone-boned refusal of a man who had spent sixty years being right about a thing no one wanted him to be right about and had not once, in sixty years, stopped being right.

*It burns the fear,* he thought, and the line came out in a voice that was not quite his own, and he held it the way a man holds a rail in a storm. *It burns the doubt, and lets the inner fire out.*

The inner fire.

He had never once, in sixty years, believed he had one. He had been the careful one, the exact one, the man of chalk and vitriol and the small geometries of scholarship. He had measured his life in margins and marginalia, and he had believed the fire in the old tales was for other men — the tall men and the bright women, the ones made for standing. But the fire at the bottom of the mountain's heart was not the fire of the old tales. It was the fire of the *holding* — the stubborn, unquenchable, ordinary fire of a man who had decided, alone, in a tower, that the record of the world was worth walking east to keep. It had been in him since the beginning. It had been in him since the tower, since the vision, since the first night he had drawn a ward in the presence of something that wanted him dead and discovered, to his surprise, that his hand did not shake.

The Cinder-Souls reached. The Magma-Eaters drank. And the inner fire, the fire that had never been lit because he had never believed it could burn, came up out of the grey emptiness of him the way the needle had come up out of the vision — not summoned, not fed, *answered*. It did not light the chamber and it did not drive the shapes back and it did not turn the tide of the labyrinth. It was a small fire. It was the fire of a single stubborn man in the middle of the world's largest darkness.

But it was his.

And the shapes of the burned — the Cinder-Souls, the ones the fire had taken — stopped. They did not retreat. They did not burn brighter. They stopped, and the reaching went out of them, and they stood in the red dark with the small fire of the stubborn man before them, and they *watched*, and the watching was not the watching of hunger. It was the watching of recognition — the look of a thing that has been in the fire and knows what the fire is for.

The fire is not for destroying. The fire is for burning away what is not true. And a man who walks through the fire with nothing left but what is true is a man the fire cannot take.

The Magma-Eaters sank back into the rivers. The Cinder-Souls stood aside the way the golems had stood aside, and the way through them opened, and the needle turned, and the red dark went on, and Eldrin Nightshade walked through the middle of the deep chambers with the small fire of himself held close and the burn of the trial at his back.

The heat burned away the doubt. That was the whole of the work, and it was the work the labyrinth had been built to do — not by the Legion, not by the hunger that had carved the runes, but by the fire itself, the old fire of the earth that had been burning since before the erasure. Every doubt he had carried into the mountain — the doubt of the summit, the doubt of the winter, the doubt of the landing — went up out of him in the heat the way the winter's snow had gone up out of the burnt valley, and what was left was the fire, and the fire was the man.

He came to the heart-chamber at the end of the galleries, and the heart-chamber was not what the old tales had promised.

It was a room of black glass at the bottom of the mountain's heart, and the runes of the hungry hand were cut into every surface, and they were lit — not with the red light of the rivers but with a violet light, the color of the underside of the sky in his vision, the color of the bruise and the wound. And at the center of the room, where the door to the deep should have stood, where the way to the stone should have opened, there was a man.

He was standing in the middle of the violet light, and he had been standing there for a long time, and he was not the hermit and he was not the whisperer and he was not any shape the old tales had ever given the dark. He was made of fire — not the honest fire of the earth, but a fire that had been *wronged*, a fire that had been light once and had been hungry for so long that the hunger had become the light. He was tall, and he was bright, and he was burning, and he was not burning out.

He looked at Eldrin, and his eyes were the eyes of a thing that had been a star and had fallen.

"Keeper," he said. "You are late. I have been waiting at this door for a season, and the waiting is the only work the stone has left me." He smiled, and the smile was a good forgery of warmth. "But you have arrived in time, after all. You are the one the stone will take. And I have been waiting for you to carry it out."

The needle turned. The violet light burned. And Eldrin Nightshade, at the bottom of the labyrinth, understood that the race he had been running since the tower had not been lost at the landing.

It had been lost at this door, a season ago, by the man who had reached it first and could not take what he had found.

But the man was still waiting. And the man needed him.

And the man was still smiling.
