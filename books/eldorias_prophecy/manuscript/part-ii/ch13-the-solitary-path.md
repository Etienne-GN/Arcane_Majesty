# Ch. 13 — The Solitary Path
<!-- Track: 7. The Solitary Path | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late spring -->

He made camp in the last of the light, at the place where the snow-line and the ash had always touched.

It was a shelf of black rock a little way inside the Fire Gate's mouth, wide enough for a man to lie on, open to the grey day on one side and closed to the heat on the other — the last country where the light of the world could reach, the border between the winter he had walked out of and the fire he had to walk into. He had reached it by instinct more than intention, his hand pressed to the chalk-bound wound in his side, his broken staff under his arm, the needle in his skull ticking east, and when he had reached it he had sat down, and the sitting down had been the first thing his body had agreed to since the landing.

He left the trusting scholar there.

That was the way he would come to think of it, in the years of the vigil: not that he had been betrayed at the Fire Gate, but that he had *left* someone there — the man who had walked a winter believing that a companion who reads the snow and splits the firewood and carries the heavier pack is a companion who can be trusted. He had left that man bleeding in the snow-line, and he did not go back for him, and the going back was not possible, because the man had never existed in the first place. He had been a figure Oren had drawn with a craftsman's patience, and the trusting scholar had been the ink.

Eldrin Nightshade, who sat on the black shelf at the mouth of the fire with his side bound and his staff broken, was not that man. He was the man who had walked out of the tower believing the record of the world could be carried by a man who was careful, and he had learned, in the space of a single landing, that the record of the world can only be carried by a man who is alone — because a man who is careful is a man who trusts, and a man who trusts is a man who can be used.

He worked. That was the discipline, and the discipline was the whole of what had survived the betrayal, and he worked the way the scholars of the tower had taught him to work: slowly, in order, with attention. He washed the wound — the chalk stopper had held, but the holding had been a stopgap, not a mend, and the wound needed the proper care of a man who means to keep living. He had water, the last of it, and he used it sparingly, and he tore a strip from the lining of his coat and bound the wound again, properly, the way he had bound a hundred sprains in the quarries under Lake Aethera, and the binding was a familiar thing, an ordinary thing, the first ordinary thing he had done since the landing, and the ordinary was a mercy.

He ate. He had almost nothing — the light things Oren had left him, professional courtesy or the last of the kindness, and he did not weigh the difference, because weighing the difference was a luxury he had spent. He ate a little, and he kept a little, and he set the rest aside against the descent.

And he planned. That was the scholar's last service to him, and he used it the way a man uses a lamp in a room he has just found to be dark. He had lost the journal — the bearings, the notes, the record of the road — and the loss was a wound of its own, the wound of a man who has spent his life keeping records suddenly without one. But he had not lost the needle. He had never once written the needle down, and that was the one act of prudence the winter had produced, and it was the act that would save him. Oren had the notes. Oren had the route, the bearings, the numbers, the map's bones. Oren did not have the compass — the living compass, the needle that knew the stone the way a man knows his own hand. The notes would carry a careful man to the door, and the door would open, and the stone would be there, and the stone would do what the stone did to unworthy hands.

The race was still on.

He turned, then, to the ledger of the winter, the way a man turns to a record he has been avoiding. It had been kept by a scholar, and it had been kept honestly, and the honesty had been the problem: every warning had been entered, and every warning had been filed under the wrong heading. The whisperer at the summit had shown him Oren's face and the black knife, and he had entered it under *the enemy's poison, refuse it* — and the entry had been the truest thing the enemy had ever said. The hermit in the green valley had watched Oren write with the cold, weighing stillness of a keeper reading a thing that has walked into his keeping uninvited, and he had entered the watching under *the old man's wariness of the outside world* — and the wariness had been the work of an eye that had seen a thousand masks and recognized the sewing on this one. And on the stair, at the landing, the footfall had changed, the stride had settled, the weight had gathered, and he had entered the settling under *the descent getting into a man* — and the descent had been getting into a man, just not the man he thought.

He had been given the truth four times — by the enemy, by the keeper, by his own senses — and he had refused it every time, because refusing it had been warmer than believing it, and warmth had been the winter's whole currency. He had spent a season buying warmth with the truth, and the betrayal had been the bill, and the bill had come due all at once, in the space of a single landing, in the country where the snow-line ended and the ash began.

He sat with the ledger open in his mind, and he did not strike the entries out, and he did not close the book. He had learned the lesson the book had been trying to teach him all winter, the lesson the whisperer had been right about in the only way that mattered: that the cracks were real, that the seams were real, that a man who reads the seams of the world for a living has no business pretending his own seams are closed. He was not going to forget the lesson, and he was not going to forgive it, and he was going to carry it down the labyrinth the way he carried the wound in his side — as a weight, a payment, the cost of the trusting scholar he had left in the snow-line.

He sat on the black shelf at the edge of the light with the plan settling in him like a foundation, and he reached, for the first time since the betrayal, for the storm.

It was not a prayer. He had never known how to pray, and the storm was not a god to be prayed to — it was a presence, a weight, the thing that had called him by name and set the needle in his skull and pointed him east. He reached for it the way a man reaches for a hand in the dark, with the whole of his attention, the way he had reached in the tower and in the vision and on every night of the walking when the doubt had come up out of the ground. And the Aether answered — the thin, strained music of the world, the same music that had been running under his life for a season — and it was there, and it was the same, and it did not answer him.

Vorgos was silent.

That was the truth of it, and he sat with the truth the way a man sits with a coin that has just proved false. He had been walking east for a season believing, in the part of him that did not file and date and weigh, that the storm was with him — that the needle was a hand, that the vision was a promise, that the voice that had called him by name would be there when the road went dark. The road had gone dark, and the voice was not there, and the needle was still there, and the needle was all there was. He did not know whether Vorgos had ever promised more. He did not know whether the storm had ever been anything but a courier, a signal-fire, a thing that had said what it had to say and gone quiet. He knew only that he was underground now, with a bound wound and a broken staff and an empty pack, and that above him, somewhere beyond the fire and the smoke and the grey sky, Vorgos watched, or did not watch, and the watching had never been the point.

*A pawn upon a cosmic board,* he thought, and the line came out in the voice of the songs, and he let it sit with him. The songs had it right, in the way songs sometimes do. A pawn does not ask the player for company. A pawn moves, and the moving is the whole of the work, and the board does not care whether the pawn is afraid.

He had been the pawn all along. He had been the pawn since the tower, since the vision, since the first night he had walked east under a sky the color of a held breath. And the winter had dressed the pawn in a friend — had given it a companion, a warmth, a reason to believe it was more than a piece on a board — and the dressing had been the work of the enemy, and the pawn had paid for the dressing with the only thing a pawn owns.

And yet. He could not quite bring himself to believe that the storm had meant him harm, or that the needle was a leash, or that the whole of the road had been a trap laid at the tower door. The trap had not been Vorgos's. The trap had been built out of his own winter, out of the warmth he had wanted, out of the companion the road had produced at the exact moment the loneliness had grown too large to carry. The storm had given him a direction and a duty; it had not given him a friend. He had gone looking for the friend himself, in the man who had caught up with him on the road, and the finding had been the trap, and the trap had been sprung by the one who had built it.

That was the thought that finally settled him, sitting on the black shelf at the edge of the light with the storm's silence above him and the needle's east below him: the betrayal had not been the storm's work, and it had not been his own, and it had been waiting for him on the road the way the dark waits for a lamp. It had been the enemy's work, done with the enemy's tools, and the only thing the enemy had needed from him was the one thing he had been all too willing to give.

He had been willing. That was the hard truth, the one that would not be filed. He had wanted the company, and the wanting had opened the door, and the man who had walked through it had been wearing the shape of the wanting. He could not blame the storm, and he could not blame the enemy for building the trap out of the oldest material in the world, and he could not blame the trusting scholar for trusting.

He could only leave the trusting scholar behind. And he had.

*The solitary path, I walk alone,* he thought, and the line came out of him the way the truth comes out of a wound, and he let it. *No friends to lose, no trust to break.*

He said it aloud, in the last of the light at the mouth of the fire, and the saying made it a vow. "I walk alone. I have no friends to lose and no trust to break. I have a needle in my skull and a wound in my side and a stone at the end of a labyrinth of fire, and I am the only one walking to it, and that is the whole of the road, and the road is enough."

It was not a bitter vow. That was the strangest thing, and he would weigh it for a long time. There was no anger in him at the road, and no self-pity, and no hunger for the vengeance the songs always promised a betrayed man. There was only a settling, a leveling, the feeling of a ship's cargo being lashed down before a long crossing. The winter had been spent carrying a weight he had not known he was carrying — the weight of the company, the weight of the wanting, the weight of a heart that had kept looking for a hand to hold while it walked toward a door that only one man could open. The betrayal had cut that weight loose. He was lighter now than he had been since the tower, and the lightness was not joy and it was not grief. It was the lightness of a man who has stopped asking the road for anything but the road.

This was the shape of the guardian, he understood. Not the tall figure of the songs, not the bright hero, not the man who stands at the door with an army behind him. The guardian is the man who stands at the door alone, who has accepted that there is no one coming to stand with him, who has stopped looking over his shoulder for a hand and started putting his own hand on the latch. The hermit had been such a man, bound to the green, keeping the valley alone for longer than the songs remembered. The man in the vision had been such a man, standing against the violet sky with his arms spread and no one behind him. The woman who had held him, and had not looked up, had been such a woman.

They had been alone, all of them. That was the shape of the work. And the work had called him, and he had answered, and the answering had cost him the winter, and the cost had been the tuition.

He gathered what was left. He stood up on the black shelf at the edge of the light, and he looked once at the country he had walked out of — the grey sky, the snow-line, the world of men and their kindnesses and their masks — and then he turned, and he walked down into the dark.

The descent was different this time. It was not the descent of the morning, the descent of a man with a companion and a plan and a heart full of the winter's warmth. It was the descent of a keeper — a man who has learned that the world is a record kept in two hands, that the first hand can be erased and the second hand cannot be lied to, and that the only trust worth keeping is the trust between a man and the work.

He went down, and the heat closed over him, and the runes burned their hungry song, and the needle turned, steady and patient, pointing east, into the labyrinth, toward the stone.

Behind him, above him, in the snow-line and the ash, the trusting scholar lay where he had left him, bleeding into the black glass, and the man who walked on did not look back.

Before him, below him, the labyrinth opened like a throat — the stairs of black glass dropping away into a red country, a country of fire and shadow and the hunger of the carved runes — and the needle turned, and the heat came up to meet him, and the road that had begun in a tower under a sky the color of a held breath ran on, through the fire, to the door.

He walked into it alone. And the aloneness, for the first time in his life, was not a weight.

It was a crown.
