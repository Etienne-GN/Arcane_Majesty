# Ch. 13 — The Solitary Path
<!-- Track: 7. The Solitary Path | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late spring -->

He made camp in the last of the light — the last light of the world, the grey glow that filtered down the stair from the mouth of the Fire Gate, thin and distant, the colour of a memory of sky.

It was a shelf of black rock a hundred paces below the first landing, wide enough for a man to lie on, open to the stair above and closed to the heat below — the last country where the light of the world could reach. He had reached it by instinct more than intention, his hand pressed to the chalk-bound wound in his side, his broken staff under his arm, the needle in his skull ticking east, and when he had reached it he had sat down, and the sitting down had been the first thing his body had agreed to since the landing.

He left the trusting scholar there.

That was the way he would come to think of it, in the years of the vigil: not that he had been betrayed at the Fire Gate, but that he had *left* someone there — the man who had walked a winter believing that a companion who reads the snow and splits the firewood and carries the heavier pack is a companion who can be trusted. He had left that man bleeding in the snow-line, and he did not go back for him, and the going back was not possible, because the man had never existed in the first place.

Eldrin Nightshade, who sat on the black shelf below the landing with his side bound and his staff broken, was not that man. He had learned, in the space of a single landing, that the record of the world can only be carried by a man who is alone.

He reached, for the first time since the betrayal, for the storm.

It was not a prayer. The storm was not a god to be prayed to — it was a presence, the thing that had called him by name and set the needle in his skull and pointed him east. He reached for it the way a man reaches for a hand in the dark, with the whole of his attention. And the Aether answered — the thin, strained music of the world — and it was there, and it was the same, and it did not answer him.

Vorgos was silent.

He had been walking east for a season believing that the storm was with him — that the needle was a hand, that the vision was a promise, that the voice that had called him by name would be there when the road went dark. The road had gone dark, and the voice was not there, and the needle was still there, and the needle was all there was. He did not know whether Vorgos had ever promised more. He knew only that he was underground now, with a bound wound and a broken staff and an empty pack, and that above him, somewhere beyond the fire and the smoke and the grey sky, Vorgos watched, or did not watch, and the watching had never been the point.

*A pawn upon a cosmic board,* he thought. The vision had it right. A pawn does not ask the player for company. A pawn moves, and the moving is the whole of the work, and the board does not care whether the pawn is afraid.

The winter had dressed the pawn in a friend — had given it a companion, a warmth, a reason to believe it was more than a piece on a board — and the dressing had been the work of the enemy, and the pawn had paid for the dressing with the only thing a pawn owns. And yet the trap had not been Vorgos's. The trap had been built out of his own winter, out of the warmth he had wanted, out of the companion the road had produced at the exact moment the loneliness had grown too large to carry. The storm had given him a direction and a duty; it had not given him a friend. He had gone looking for the friend himself, and the finding had been the trap, and the trap had been sprung by the one who had built it.

He had been willing. That was the hard truth, the one that would not be filed. He had wanted the company, and the wanting had opened the door, and the man who had walked through it had been wearing the shape of the wanting. He could not blame the storm, and he could not blame the enemy for building the trap out of the oldest material in the world, and he could not blame the trusting scholar for trusting.

He could only leave the trusting scholar behind. And he had.

*The solitary path, I walk alone,* he thought, and the line came out of him the way the truth comes out of a wound, and he let it. *No friends to lose, no trust to break.*

He said it aloud, in the last of the light below the landing, and the saying made it a vow. "I walk alone. I have no friends to lose and no trust to break. I have a needle in my skull and a wound in my side and a stone at the end of a labyrinth of fire, and I am the only one walking to it, and that is the whole of the road, and the road is enough."

His voice broke on the last word. He let it break.

It was not a bitter vow. There was no anger in him at the road, and no self-pity, and no hunger for the vengeance the old tales always promised a betrayed man. There was only a settling, the feeling of a ship's cargo being lashed down before a long crossing. The betrayal had cut the weight loose. He was lighter now than he had been since the tower, and the lightness was not joy and it was not grief. It was the lightness of a man who has stopped asking the road for anything but the road.

This was the shape of the guardian, he understood. Not the tall figure of the old tales, not the bright hero, not the man who stands at the door with an army behind him. The guardian is the man who stands at the door alone, who has stopped looking over his shoulder for a hand and started putting his own hand on the latch.

He gathered what was left. He stood up on the black shelf below the landing, and he looked once at the stair above — the grey glow thinning, the country of men and their kindnesses and their masks growing smaller with every step — and then he turned, and he walked down into the dark.

The descent was different this time. It was not the descent of the morning, the descent of a man with a companion and a plan and a heart full of the winter's warmth. It was the descent of a keeper — a man who has learned that the only trust worth keeping is the trust between a man and the work.

He went down, and the heat closed over him, and the runes burned their hungry hum, and the needle turned, steady and patient, pointing east, into the labyrinth, toward the stone.

Above him, in the snow-line and the ash, the trusting scholar lay where he had left him, bleeding into the black glass at the first landing, and the man who walked on did not look back.

Below him, the labyrinth opened like a throat — the stairs of black glass dropping away into a red country, a country of fire and shadow and the hunger of the carved runes — and the needle turned, and the heat came up to meet him, and the road that had begun in a tower under a sky the colour of a held breath ran on, through the fire, to the door.

He walked into it alone. And the aloneness, for the first time in his life, was not a weight.

It was a crown.
