# Ch. 11 — The Fire Gate
<!-- Track: 5-6 | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, spring -->

They left the valley on the third morning, and the leaving was harder than Eldrin had expected it to be.

The hermit did not walk them out. He stood at the door of the grown hut with the green light coming down around him, and he looked at Eldrin, and he said, "The sight is yours now. Open it when the road goes dark, and let it be the last thing you trust, and the first thing you spend." And he looked at Oren, standing a little apart with his chronicle under his arm, and the river-colored eyes went cold for a moment, and he said nothing, and the saying nothing was the whole of the sentence.

Then the forest opened a path for them, and they walked east, and the green closed behind them like a door that had decided it was done with them.

The spring was waiting on the far side of the mountains. That was the impossible gift of the season — that the world had caught up with the valley while they were inside it. The snow-line had retreated up the slopes while the forest had held them; the lowlands had broken open into the ordinary riot of a spring that had been waiting all winter to be let in. The air warmed as they walked. The rivers ran full and loud. The birds returned, and the birdsong had the particular quality of a thing that has just remembered how to sing, and Eldrin walked through it with the sight open at the edge of his attention and the needle in his skull pointing east, and for the first time in months the walking did not feel like a burden. It felt like the road was carrying them.

Oren's questions grew more careful as the country warmed.

It was not that he asked about the stone outright. The mask was too good for that, and Eldrin, looking back much later, would be able to date the exact moment the mask began to thin. Oren asked about the *route*. He asked about the old roads, the ones the maps no longer carried, and whether the country east of the mountains had a shape in the old chronicles. He asked about the compass — not the mental needle, which Eldrin had never spoken of, but the brass one that lived in Eldrin's pack, the fine thing the college had given him for his fortieth year, and whether it had served him well on the long road. He asked, with the particular innocent appetite of a scholar for whom every fact is a coin, about the *bearings* — the directions a man would need if he were to walk from the mountains to the old court of Eldoria without a guide, the landmarks, the line of the road, the country between here and the ruin.

And Eldrin answered. That was the whole tragedy of it, and he would not be able to point to the moment it became a tragedy, because it did not become a tragedy in a moment. It became a tragedy the way a road becomes a road — one step, then another, then a direction. He shared what he had filed. He drew the route in the dust by a campfire — the mountain's shoulder, the pass, the line of the road east. He read aloud, once, from the second journal, the bearings he had copied down in the tower with such care — the coordinates the storm had burned into him, translated into the ordinary language of maps, the numbers a man could carry without carrying the needle. He did it because Oren was a chronicler and chroniclers collect such things. He did it because the man had carried the heavier pack and split the firewood and fought a ghost wearing his own face. He did it because the trust had been rebuilt around a fire, and because the crack was closed, or nearly closed, and because a man who has been tested by a whisperer of doubt is not eager to go on testing the only warmth he has.

He shared the numbers. He did not share the needle. That was the one line he never crossed, and it was the line that would save him.

It was not that he was unaware of the giving. The scholar in him was aware of everything, and the scholar in him weighed every question Oren asked with a small private alarm that had grown quieter each day, the way any alarm grows quiet when the fire it is ringing for never comes. Oren asked about the road east, and Eldrin told him what the road was. Oren asked about the old court of Eldoria, and Eldrin told him what the records said, and what the records did not say, and what the canticles hinted at. Oren asked about the *bearing* — the exact line of it, the numbers, the way a man would know he was walking true without a compass that pointed at the north — and Eldrin heard the question, and weighed it, and answered it, because it was a scholar's question, and Oren was a scholar, and the numbers were already written in the journal that lay in the top of Eldrin's pack every night, open to the world.

He noticed, once, that Oren's chronicle had begun to fill with more than the road's stories — that pages of the grey quick handwriting had appeared that were not about the villages and the passes and the hermit's valley but about *routes*, about the country east, about the shape of a journey that had a destination. He noticed, and he filed it, and he told himself that a chronicler records the road he walks, and that a man who has walked a season with another man has a right to the shape of the walking.

He did not open the sight on Oren again. That was the line he crossed in the other direction, the line that cost him more than the numbers. The sight would have shown him what the hermit had seen — the wrongness at the edge of the life-thread, the weighing, the part of the man that had been set down years ago and never picked up. He did not open it. He had opened it once, in the valley, and he had told himself the death-threads were bleeding through, and he had not opened it again, and the not-opening was the last gift he gave to the mask before the mask came off.

They came to the Fire Gate on the seventh day of spring.

The land had been changing for a day before they reached it — the green thinning, the soil darkening, the air taking on a grain, a warmth that was not the sun's. The rivers ran warmer. The trees grew stunted, and then bare, and then the ground itself began to show its bones: black stone, ridged and scorched, the skin of a country that had been burned once and had not quite grown back. The compass in Eldrin's pack swung slowly, uselessly, pointing at the ground. And the needle in his skull — the true needle, the one the storm had planted — began to spin.

He had felt it tremble before. He had felt it go steady and urgent in the pass, and quiet and listening in the valley. He had never felt it spin. It spun now, a slow complete rotation, as if the thing it pointed at had grown too large to be contained by a direction, as if east were no longer a place but a *presence*, filling the horizon the way the sea fills the edge of a coast. It spun, and the spinning was not confusion. The spinning was the sound a needle makes when the compass finally understands what it has been pointing at.

The Fire Gate rose out of the black stone at the head of a valley that had no green in it at all. It was not a gate in the sense of a door. It was a gate in the sense of a *mouth* — a black archway cut into the mountain's flank, tall as the tower that had held his study for thirty years, and beyond it the dark went down, and out of the dark came heat. He felt it before he reached it, a dry radiating warmth like the warmth of a stove that has been burning all day, and the air at the threshold was the air of a place where the snow had never fallen and the ash had never stopped settling. The ground under the arch was black glass. The walls beside it were scored with runes — not his runes, not scholar's runes, a cruder hand, a hungrier hand, cut deep and unpolished, the runes of a thing that had carved them to feed.

He stopped at the threshold, and he did not open the sight, and the not-opening cost him a clarity he would have paid anything for later. It did not need the sight. The runes were cut in the same pattern he had read in the frozen camp at the summit, in the violet crystals growing out of the Crag-Fiend's hide — the same hungry geometry, the same signature of a thing that had passed this way and left its hunger behind like a receipt. Whoever had cut these runes had not cut them to ward. They had cut them to *advertise* — to tell the dark that something of value had passed this way, and to tell anyone who came behind that they were expected.

The needle spun. The heat came up out of the dark. And Eldrin stood at the mouth of the way down and understood, with the clarity that comes at the threshold of a long descent, that the country he had walked toward for a season had finally found him.

Oren had stopped beside him, a half-step back, looking up at the arch with his pack settling on his shoulders and his face doing nothing at all. "The old maps call this the road to the court," he said. His voice was level, the voice of a man reading a description back. "They say the court was burned — not by war, by *maintenance*. The kings who built it made it to stand, and the ones who came after made it to burn, and the burn is still going." He looked at Eldrin, and the grey eyes were steady, and there was something in them that had never quite been there before — a satisfaction, a settling, the look of a man who has just confirmed the last fact on a long list. "We're close now. I can feel it."

"You can feel it," Eldrin said.

"The heat. The road turning." Oren shrugged, a small easy movement. "The country knows what it's carrying. So do I. I've been walking toward this for a long time — longer than you know." He smiled, and the smile was the old smile, the whole one, and for a moment Eldrin saw only the man who had read the snow and split the firewood and carried the heavier pack. "Come on. Let's not stand at the mouth of the dark discussing it. We'll camp here tonight, and we'll walk in at dawn, and we'll find out together whether the old court is worth the walking."

It was sensible. It was the kind of practical, woodcraft-solid advice Oren had been giving all winter, the kind that had kept them alive. Eldrin agreed, and they made camp on the black glass at the mouth of the gate, and the sun went down beyond the burnt valley, and the heat came up out of the dark, and the two of them sat by a fire that had never needed to be built because the ground itself was warm.

Oren was quiet that night. That was not unusual — the approach to a descent made any man quiet — but the quiet had a different grain to it, a quality of weighing, of a thing being settled. He banked the fire. He checked his pack, and then he checked it again, which was not like him. And when the dark was complete and the heat was steady and the gate stood black against the deeper black, he looked across the fire at Eldrin, and the grey eyes were careful, and the question he asked was the one Eldrin had been expecting since the foot of the mountains.

Eldrin, in the interval, had been reading the runes. Not with the sight — with the eyes of his trade, the ones that had spent forty years reading the world's small print. The runes at the gate's mouth were cut deep and set in lines that marched into the dark, and they were not a language he knew, but they were a *voice* he recognized, the same voice the violet crystals had spoken with at the summit, the same hunger wearing the same grammar. Whatever was down there knew the road was coming. Whatever was down there had been expecting him.

"Eldrin," Oren said. "What will you do when you find it?"

Eldrin looked at the fire. He had asked himself the question a hundred times, and he had never once answered it aloud, and the answer had been the same every time. "Guard it," he said. "That's all it's for. I've had the whole of this road to learn that, and I learned it in the green valley from a man who tried to guard it himself and failed." He looked up at Oren across the fire. "The stone isn't a weapon. It isn't a door, and it isn't a key. It's a stopper — the thing that holds the world shut at the one seam where it could come open. My work is to see that it keeps holding, and to be there when the holding is tested, and to stay until the holding doesn't need me anymore. There's no glory in it. There's no end to it, the way the songs mean. It's a vigil, and I'm the one the storm sent to keep it."

He had expected something. A nod. A silence. The comfortable weight of a thing shared. What he got was a smile.

Oren smiled across the fire, the same open, generous smile, the smile of a man who has been given a story and is savoring it — and the smile did not reach his eyes. It was the first time Eldrin had seen that, the whole smile and the unwhole eyes, and he saw it, and he filed it, and he did not know what to file it under, because the file he would have used was the file he had sealed on the descent, the file marked *the crack*, and he had spent a spring pretending the crack was closed.

"It's a vigil," Oren said, softly. "That's what you'll do. You'll guard it." He looked at Eldrin for a long moment, and the grey eyes were very steady, and the fire moved between them. "And you'll be very good at it, I think. That's the thing about you, Eldrin. You were made for holding things that don't ask to be held." He banked the fire, the small domestic gesture, and he lay down, and he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said, into the dark, "I'll be right there with you, though. Down into whatever's under this mountain. I didn't walk this far to leave you at the last door."

The words were the right words. They were, Eldrin thought, exactly the right words, and they sat in the firelight the way the right words always sat, and the trust stood up in him and took its breath, and the smile he had seen — the whole smile and the unwhole eyes — settled into the file marked *the crack* and was joined by a date.

They slept at the mouth of the Fire Gate, in the warmth of the burnt earth, with the gate standing black against the deeper black and the heat coming up out of the country below. The needle in Eldrin's skull went on spinning, slow and steady, patient as a tide. And somewhere beneath them, in the labyrinth of fire and ash, the thing that had carved the runes and fed the heat was waiting — and the man sleeping a few feet away with his chronicle under his arm was not the only thing in the world, that night, wearing a face he had not been born with.
