# Ch. 12 — Treachery's Bite
<!-- Track: 6. Treachery's Bite | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late spring -->

Dawn came grey over the burnt valley, and they went down into the dark.

That was the order of the morning: they woke at the mouth of the Fire Gate, they packed, and they went down. The transition was so ordinary that Eldrin would relive it for the rest of the road — the way a life can be arranged so neatly around a single instant, the way the instant itself arrives wearing the face of every other instant. Oren checked the straps on his pack. Eldrin checked the second journal in its oilcloth, the one with the bearings in it, and settled it in the top of his pack where it had lived all winter. The sun came up over the rim of the burnt valley. The gate's black mouth swallowed them, and the heat closed over them like a hand, and the snow-line — the last white of the mountains, the last of the world they had walked out of — went out of sight behind them like a country sinking.

The descent was a stair of black glass, worn by feet that had used it for centuries, and the runes ran beside it in lines, and the runes were louder in the dark — they held the heat, they held the hunger, they gave the darkness a voice that climbed toward the light. Eldrin walked a step ahead, reading the way the stairs turned, the needle spinning in his skull, the heat coming up to meet them. He did not open the sight. That was the last thing he did not do, and he would carry it the way the fourteen dead had carried their camp — frozen in the middle of the act of living, mid-step, mid-breath, unaware.

He felt the wrongness first. That was the true order of it — not the blow, not the pain, not the black knife. A wrongness, at the edge of the awareness, the way the grain of the world's silence had felt wrong in Thaloria before the letter came. It was Oren's footfall. That was the detail he would date, in the small hours, for the rest of the road: Oren's footfall had changed. In a winter of walking, Oren had walked a half-step behind him, the stride of a man reading the country, patient, unhurried. On the stair, at the landing, the stride had gone a half-step *too* close — a settling, a gathering, the way a man's weight gathers before a blow. Eldrin's hand was on the staff; the staff was always there; and the hand had begun, of its own accord, to tighten.

He did not turn. He did not look. He had learned, in sixty years, to trust the wrongness — the draft under the door, the grain in the silence — and he had also learned that a man who lives by the wrongness and cannot name it will eventually spend his life flinching at shadows. He told himself it was the heat. He told himself it was the runes, the hunger of the carved walls, the descent getting into a man. He did not open the sight, and the not-opening was the last decision he made as the man who trusted, and he was still making it when the landing opened wide enough for two men to stand, and the footfall settled behind him, and the world went sideways.

Oren struck at the first landing.

There was no warning. There was no sign. One moment the stairs opened onto a shelf of black rock wide enough for two men to stand, and the next the world was going sideways — a blow, a weight, a bright white noise of pain in his side that arrived before the understanding of it, and Eldrin's legs went out from under him and he came down on the black glass with his pack beneath him and the air gone out of his lungs, and he looked up at Oren standing over him with a knife in his hand.

The knife was not the knife Oren had carried all winter. The knife Oren had carried all winter had been a real thing, bright steel, honest, the blade of a man who split firewood and read snow. This knife was black. It was not painted; it was *made* of the black — a darkness that stood out against the dark the way a wound stands out against a body, a blade of the same stuff the vision had shown him at the summit, in the whisperer's window, in the moment he had refused to believe. It dripped. Not blood — the dark the knife was made of ran off it in threads, the way the wrongness had run off the Frost-Shades, and the threads reached toward the runes on the wall as if they were coming home.

Eldrin lay on the black glass with his hand pressed to his side and the blood going out between his fingers and understood, with the terrible clean clarity that comes at the bottom of a betrayal, what the whisperer had shown him.

It had been true. All of it — the snow-line, the ash, the black knife, the patience in Oren's eyes. The whisperer of doubt had told him the truth once, dressed in the shape of a lie, and he had refused it because refusing it had been kinder than believing it, and the kindness had cost him his side and his pack and the whole of his trust.

"Treachery's bite, a sudden sting," Oren said. He was not smiling. That was the strangest thing, the detail Eldrin would carry — the mask had come off, and there was no satisfaction under it, no triumph, nothing but a careful professional flatness, the look of a man doing a job he has done before. "The dagger that the shadows bring." He looked down at Eldrin, and the grey eyes were the same grey eyes, and they were empty of the man who had worn them. "You knew, didn't you. Part of you. The whisperer showed you at the summit, and you chose not to believe it, because believing it would have meant the winter was a lie." He shook his head, a small almost-gentle motion. "It was the best work I ever did, Eldrin. I want you to know that. Twenty years of masks, and you were the first one I ever had to *be*."

"Oren," Eldrin said. The word came out wet. His side was burning. "Oren, whatever they've told you, whatever you think you're doing —"

"My name isn't Oren."

The sentence landed the way a door lands. Eldrin had known it, in the same way he had known the knife was black, and the knowing did not help. "I don't have another name for you," he said. "I had the one you gave me."

"You had the one I *wore*," Oren said. "That's all it ever was. A chronicler who collects lost libraries — that was a good one. I kept it for years before I met you, the way you keep a key for a door you haven't found yet. And then you came walking down the road with the record of the world in your satchel and the storm's compass in your skull, and I knew I'd found the door." He looked at the knife in his hand, and the black ran off it in threads. "I'm not here for the libraries, Eldrin. I never was. I'm here for the stone — the Anchor, the Heartstone, the thing that holds the Lock shut. My master wants it. Not to guard it. To *break the seal*. To let the dark in that you've spent a season walking to keep out."

"Your master," Eldrin said. The words were hard now; his side was going cold. "The whisperer said there were three of you hunting. Which one owns you?"

Oren's face did a thing Eldrin had never seen it do — it flickered, a break in the professional flatness, something that might have been pride and might have been fear. "The one the other two underestimate," he said. "The one who has been waiting the longest. He doesn't whisper in storms and he doesn't burn in the fire. He moves in the quiet, and he sends his people in ahead of him, and by the time you hear his name it's the last thing you hear. They call him the Silent Executioner. Voraun." He said the name the way a man says a thing he has been saving up for years. "You'll meet him, at the end of the road. That's the whole of the plan, Eldrin — you're going to walk the stone out of the ruin for him, and he's going to be standing at the door when you do."

"And you," Eldrin said. The words were hard now; his side was going cold. "You walk ahead of him. You learn the road. You make the masks."

"I am the masks," Oren said. There was no shame in it. There was no triumph either. There was only the flat professional tone, the tone of a man describing the only trade he had ever been taught. "I've been Voraun's for thirty years. Before that I was no one's — a boy who was good at being believed, which is the same as being no one, in a world that believes in nothing else. My master found me, and he gave the skill a purpose, and the purpose has a shape, and the shape is the road we walk." He looked at Eldrin, and for a moment the grey eyes were almost warm, and the warmth was the worst thing of all. "I was not lying to you. That's the trick of the best masks, Eldrin — you learn to make them out of true things. I do collect lost libraries. I do love the old kings and their records and the way the world forgets. I split your firewood because splitting it was a pleasure, and I carried your pack because carrying it was a kindness, and I meant every kindness, the way a man means a kindness he will later have to spend." He shook his head, a small motion, almost rueful. "The scholarship was real. The friendship —" He stopped. "The friendship was a job. But it was the best job I ever drew, and I'll remember it."

The words landed in Eldrin like stones, one after another, each one finding its place in the ledger of the winter: the split firewood, the carried pack, the thousand sheep, the smile that had not reached his eyes at the gate. All real. All part of the work. A mask made of true things is the hardest mask to take off, and the one Eldrin had worn all winter had been Oren's finest — because Eldrin had not worn it. Oren had. Oren had been the mask, warm and whole and unhurried, and the man underneath had been watching him the whole time, weighing him, waiting for the door.

"You're going to kill me now," Eldrin said. "That's not a plan that carries the stone anywhere."

"No," Oren said. "I'm not going to kill you. You're too valuable for that." He crouched, and the black knife came down, and Eldrin's hand went to the staff — the staff was there, it was always there — and the runes came up with a light that was already too slow and too weak, and the black knife met the blue light, and the blue light went dark where they touched, the way the Frost-Shades had gone dark against the ward, only wrong, only inverted.

And then Oren was no longer holding the knife. The dark had come up his arm — the threads running up the wrist, the forearm, the elbow, the shoulder — and the arm was not an arm anymore, it was a *blade*, black and unblinking, the shadow-bite of his master's touch grown out of his own flesh, and the blade swept down, and the blue light of the staff broke, and the staff broke, and the runes died along the split wood like a line of lights going out.

Eldrin tried the sight. That was the last weapon he had, the gift of the green valley, and he tried to open it in the middle of the landing with his side going out from under him — and it opened, and it showed him Oren. Not the mask. The man. The life-thread the hermit had taught him to read, and at its edge the wrongness he had seen in the hut and refused to name — not a death-thread, a *settling*, a part of the man that had been set down so long ago that the rest of him had grown over it, the way a scar grows over a wound. And he understood, in the single terrible instant before the black blade came down, what the hermit had seen that night and what he had chosen not to say: the hermit had read Oren the way Eldrin was reading him now, and the hermit had known, and the saying nothing had been the hermit's gift — a warning too late to use, too true to speak aloud, left for Eldrin to find on his own.

The sight did not save him. It had never been a weapon; it was an eye, and an eye does not stop a blade. But it gave him one thing, in the instant before the world went white: it showed him that the man swinging the black blade at him was not the man who had read the snow. That man had never existed. The man swinging the blade was the only man there had ever been, and the winter had been the work, and the work had been the man.

The black blade came down again. Eldrin rolled — he was on the black glass, he had nothing but the ruined staff and his own failing side, and he rolled the way a man rolls when the only alternative is not rolling, and the blade went past him into the glass where he had been, and the glass cracked and the crack filled with black, and he came up against the wall with the runes burning their hungry heat against his back.

"It's nothing personal," Oren said. He stood over him, and the blade-arm was very still, and the grey eyes were very calm. "That's the truth of it. You were the assignment. You were the best assignment I ever drew, and I'll remember this road for the rest of my life, but you were the assignment, and the assignment has a schedule." He looked at Eldrin, and for a moment — one moment — there was something in the grey eyes that was almost the man who had read the snow. "I'd have been glad of you as a friend. That's the part that's true. But the work comes first, and the work was never yours."

He moved. Eldrin did not see the blow land — he saw the black arm start, and then the world was a bright white noise again, and his side was on fire, and the fire had a new wound in it, and he was on the ground with the black glass against his cheek and the runes humming against his back and the blood going out of him the way the Aether had gone out of the pass.

He heard Oren's pack open. He heard the oilcloth. He heard the sound of the second journal being drawn out — the small, familiar sound of a book leaving its place, the sound he had made himself a thousand times in the tower, the sound a reader makes when a reader is done with a page.

"The bearings," Oren said, from very far away. "The numbers you read me at the fire. Good. That's all I needed. The rest I'll walk." A pause. The heat moved. "The compass — the real one, the one in your skull — that stays with you. You can't give it to me, and I can't take it, and I knew that the day I met you. But a needle only tells you where the stone is. It doesn't walk the road for you. And I have the road now." Another pause. The black glass was very cold against Eldrin's cheek, and the heat was very loud, and the two of them were the strangest mixture of cold and hot a man could die in. "Goodbye, Eldrin. I'll tell Voraun you walked it all the way to the door. It's the least I can do for the best assignment I ever drew."

The footsteps went away. The dark swallowed them. The runes burned. And Eldrin lay on the black glass at the first landing of the Fire Gate, in the place where the snow-line had ended and the ash had begun, with his side open and his staff broken and his trust in ashes, and the needle in his skull — the only compass he had ever truly owned — turned, slow and steady, patient as a tide, pointing east, to the country below.

He did not know how long he lay there.

The heat held him. That was the mercy and the cruelty of the place — the ground was warm, and the warmth did what warmth does, it kept the cold out, and the cold was the thing that would have finished him cleanly. Instead the wound in his side went on being a wound, and the blood went on going out, and the runes went on with their hungry hum, and the needle went on pointing, and somewhere below him the man he had trusted — the man who had read the snow and split the firewood and carried the heavier pack and fought a ghost wearing his own face — was walking east into the labyrinth, carrying the record of the world in his pack.

It was the journal that saved him. He would not have believed it, lying there in the heat with his life going out of him, but it was the journal, and it was the habit of the scholar, and it was the last of the trust. His hand had gone to his side, and his side had gone numb, and his hand had come away, and the hand had fallen — not to the glass, to the *pack*, to the ruin of his pack, and his fingers had found the oilcloth, and the oilcloth had been slit, and the journal had been taken, and beneath the journal, at the bottom of the pack where he had always kept it, his fingers had found the last thing Oren had not thought to take: the rune-kit. The chalk. The vitriol. The quicksilver. The small tools of a life that had been spent learning that a man who is careful can sometimes close a wound the world has opened.

He drew. He did not have the sight in him, or the magic, or the strength — he had the *habit*, and the habit was enough. He drew the stopper, the lock, the knot, the figure the masters had taught him, the one that says *here the world changes hands*, and he drew it over the wound in his side, and he fed it, and the feeding was a wrenching — the last of the winter's soul going out of him the way water goes out of a cracked jug — and the blue light came up out of the chalk lines, thin and dying, and it did not close the wound and it did not stop the blood and it did not heal anything.

But it held. It held the way the stopper held the bottle, the way the stone held the seam. It held the space in which the blood could slow and the body could decide whether to keep living, and the decision, in the end, was yes.

He lay there a long time. The runes hummed. The heat held him. The wound in his side had gone from burning to a dull, settled ache, the ache of a thing that has decided not to kill him after all — or has agreed to wait and see. And in the dark, in the heat, at the bottom of the first landing of the Fire Gate, with the blood dried black against his side and the ruin of his staff beside him, Eldrin Nightshade took the ledger of his life and turned to the page he had been avoiding all winter, and he read it.

Oren was not a friend. Oren had never been a friend. The winter had been an assignment, and he had been the assignment, and every warmth in it had been the work of a craftsman. The whisperer had shown him the truth at the summit, and he had refused it. The hermit had seen the truth in the hut, and he had not named it. And he — he had walked into the Fire Gate with the record of the world in his pack and the door open in his heart, and he had paid for the opening with his side, and the payment was not finished.

He did not weep. The weeping would come later, or it would not come at all — he did not know which, and the not-knowing was part of the wound. What he did was the only thing the scholar in him knew to do with a page that had been proved false: he struck it out. He took the winter, the split firewood, the carried pack, the thousand sheep, the grey eyes that had read the snow, and he drew the line through them in his own ledger, and the line was the hardest figure he had ever drawn, and he drew it clean.

His hand was shaking. He let it shake.

He did not climb. That was the first thing the scholar in him understood, sitting on the black glass with the wound bound and the staff broken: the way back was not the way forward. The stairs behind him rose toward the grey light and the snow-line and the country of men, and the country of men was where Oren had walked, and where Oren had walked was a road that led only to the door at the end of the labyrinth — the door Voraun was holding, the door that waited for the hand the stone would accept. The needle in his skull turned, steady and patient, pointing east, into the dark of the Fire Gate, into the labyrinth, toward the stone. The journal was gone. The bearings, the numbers, the careful map — gone. But the needle was not gone. The needle had never once been written down, and that was the one act of prudence the winter had produced, and it was the act that would save him. Oren had the notes. Oren had the route. Oren did not have the compass — the living compass, the needle that knew the stone the way a man knows his own hand.

The race was still on.

He gathered what was left. The food: enough for a day, if he was careful — Oren had left him the light things, the ones a wounded man could carry, and the leaving had been professional courtesy or the last of the kindness, and he did not weigh the difference. The rune-kit: intact, the chalk and the vitriol and the quicksilver, the tools of a trade he had never once used to close a wound and had just used to close one. The staff — what was left of it, a hand-span of broken wood with the runes dead along the split. He used it as a crutch, and the wood bit into his palm, and the palm was the same palm the runes had burned in the pass, and the two wounds kept each other company.

The needle turned. Eldrin Nightshade stood up on the black glass at the first landing of the Fire Gate, and he looked east, and he looked down, and he made the choice the hermit had made for him and the whisperer had tried to make against him and the man he had trusted had made for him with a black blade: he chose the road.

The blue light died. The runes burned. The needle turned. And Eldrin Nightshade walked down into the dark of the Fire Gate, wounded, betrayed, broken, alive, and began the descent he had been sent to make — alone.

Behind him, above him, in the snow-line and the ash, the trusting scholar lay where he had left him, bleeding into the black glass. And ahead of him, below him, somewhere in the labyrinth's heat, the man who had worn the name Oren walked on — and the door was waiting, and the one who had waited the longest stood before it, patient as a tide.
