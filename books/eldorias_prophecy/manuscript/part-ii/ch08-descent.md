# Ch. 8 — Descent
<!-- Track: 4. Summit of Despair | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late winter -->

The mountain was harder to leave than it had been to climb.

That was the truth the songs never carried. The climb had a shape to it — a summit, an end, a country to win. The descent had nothing but the weight. Every step down was a step into a lower, warmer, more ordinary world, and the ordinary world was the thing Eldrin had been walking away from all winter, and now he was being asked to walk back toward it carrying everything he had seen on the way out.

The toll was physical first. That was the merciful order of it. His hands — the chalk-holding, page-turning hands — had been burned by the runes' light, a deep violaceous scorch up the palms that would not stop hurting, and the hurt was the Snap's cousin, the bill for a magic that had been spent at the wrong altitude in the wrong weather for the wrong reasons. The world had gone grey around him, and his heart was doing the thing it had done after the first ward — beating as if it were trying to remember a rhythm it had almost forgotten. He was cold in a way that was not the cold of the mountain but the cold of the body running on its last reserves, the cold of a lamp with a thread of oil left.

And the mind followed the body down, because a tired mind will follow a tired body anywhere. The vision of the storm came back at every halt — Oren's face, the snow-line, the ash, the black knife. It came back in the white hours, in the pauses for breath, in the small still moments when the descent's rhythm let him think, and each time it came back he filed it, and each time he filed it the filing was a little harder, a little more like the work of hiding a thing rather than keeping it.

Oren noticed.

He noticed the way he noticed everything — by reading the man the way he read snow, by the small deviations, the held silences, the compass checks that Eldrin no longer bothered to disguise. On the third morning of the descent, Oren sat down across from him in the lee of a rock and did not pick up his pack, and the not-picking-up was a question in a language Eldrin had been dodging for two days.

"You've been quiet," Oren said. Not accusatory. Just a man stating the weather. "Quieter than the mountain deserves. And you keep touching your side where the runes burned, and you keep looking east, and you keep not telling me something." He waited. The wind moved. "I asked you at the top what the whisperer showed you, and you gave me the archive and the college and the years. All true things. I've no doubt they're all true. But a chronicler can tell when a man is carrying a story he isn't telling — and I think, after the pass, you're carrying something heavier than the archive."

The lie came out of Eldrin before he had decided to tell it. That was the way of lies, in his experience — they arrived the way the doubt had arrived at the summit, quietly, dressed in the voice of reason. "He showed me the end of the road," he said. "The ruin, the stone, the door. He showed me the way it would go wrong — the way it ends for a man who walks to the end of the world for a thing he doesn't fully understand." He made himself meet Oren's eyes. "That's all. It was a picture of my own failure. He's very good at pictures."

Oren looked at him for a long moment. The grey eyes were steady, and there was something in them that Eldrin could not read — and could not read, in that moment, because he did not want to, because reading it might have required him to stop lying, and the lie was the only thing between him and the splinter. "And the road you're walking," Oren said, "is still the road you told me about? The ruins, the old stones, the thing you mean to see found or see kept lost?"

"It's the same road," Eldrin said. "It's the only road."

Oren nodded. And because he was the man he was — the man who read snow and split firewood and carried the heavier pack — he did not press it further. He stood, and he shouldered his pack, and he said, "Then we'll walk it. And when we get there, I'll be beside you, and we'll see what the end of the road has to say for itself." He grinned, the old open grin, and the grin was a real thing, unhurried and whole, and Eldrin felt the trust go back into place like a joint being reset — and he knew, with the part of him that filed and dated and weighed, that the reset was a deception, that a joint that has been broken does not go back to what it was, that the crack was still there, closed, hidden, waiting.

But the warmth of the grin was real. And the descent was long. And a man who is cold will take warmth wherever it is offered, even from a fire he does not quite believe in.

The crack widened in the walking. That was the terrible arithmetic of it — that a lie, once told, does not sit still; it grows its own root system, and every true thing afterward has to grow around it. Eldrin found himself guarding smaller and smaller things. The bearings in the second journal, the ones he had copied down in the tower with such care, now seemed to him the kind of thing a man should keep folded small in his mind and not inked on paper where a chronicler's eyes might drift. He caught himself, on the fourth morning, drawing the day's route in the frost with the toe of his boot and scuffing it out again before Oren had finished rolling his bedroll — and the scuffing was a sentence he had never intended to write, a whole language of mistrust that had grown up between two men walking the same direction.

He watched Oren more. It was involuntary, and it was exhausting, and it was a betrayal of the trust all on its own — because a man who watches a friend for signs is no longer a friend, he is a reader, and the friend can feel the reading even when he cannot name it. Oren's questions, when they came, came with the old ease, the chronicler's appetite; but Eldrin heard them now through the memory of the vision, through the splinter, and every question about the country ahead — *what lies beyond the foothills, have you a route in mind, is it much further to the place the old maps call the fire* — arrived at his ear dressed in a question of a different kind.

*He asked one question too many,* the small voice said, in the voice he used for heresy. *At the foot of the mountains, when the fire was warm and the weariness was deep, he asked exactly where to press. And you dismissed it. And the whisperer showed you the answer, and you dismissed that too.*

He dismissed it now. He had to. He was a scholar, and the evidence said Oren was what he appeared to be — the man who read the snow, the man who carried the heavier pack, the man who had fought a ghost wearing his own face. The evidence was overwhelming. The evidence was all he had.

And still the crack widened, because the crack was not in the evidence. The crack was in the trust, and the trust had been tested by a whisperer of doubt, and a thing that has been tested does not come back the same.

They came down out of the snow into the foothills on the fifth day, and they made camp in the ruin of a shepherd's fold — the same kind of lean-to, the same ash-ringed hearth, the same wind working at the edges of the roof. Oren built the fire up properly, the way he did everything, and the fire was the first real warmth they had had in a week, and they sat on opposite sides of it with the dusk coming down around the fold and the last of the mountain's white going pale in the east, and the warmth did the thing warmth does.

"Eldrin," Oren said, "have I ever told you about the library that walked over the mountains?"

He had not. It came out in the firelight the way Oren's best stories came out — unpressed, unhurried, the way a man tells a story he has told a hundred times and still loves. Before the Gap, before the tidying, before the kings whose courts the songs barely remember, there had been a library in the eastern country — not a collection, a *world*, room after room of the record as it was actually kept, the true handwriting of the age. And when the dark came down out of the north, the librarians had done what the songs never think to record: they had refused to burn it. They had taken the books down off the shelves, book by book, and they had wrapped each one in oilcloth, and they had set them on the backs of a thousand sheep and driven the sheep over the mountains into the country the dark had not reached.

"A thousand sheep," Oren said, "carrying the world on their backs. The librarians walked beside them for a month, in the snow, reading nothing, writing nothing, just *walking* with the record of everything. And when they got to the far side, and the dark came down over the mountains behind them, they unpacked the books in a cave and lit a fire, and they sat with the whole of their world on the shelves around them, and they began, very calmly, to copy it out again — because the originals were old and the dark had been close, and a record that exists in only one hand is a record that can be erased."

The fire moved. The wind sang its thin note over the fold. And Eldrin, who had spent his life in small rooms of proof, who had built his whole understanding on the erasure of records and the tidying of shelves, heard the story land in him the way a stone lands in still water, and he could not speak for a moment.

"What happened to the library?" he asked, when he could.

"The mountain it went over isn't on any map," Oren said. "That's the whole of the story. No one remembers the name of the mountain, or the cave, or the hand that copied the last book. The dark got some of it — everyone says the dark got some of it, that's the part the songs remember, the burning. But a thousand sheep walked east with the rest, and the east is a big country, and a chronicler who believes in lost libraries believes that some of those books are still walking." He looked across the fire at Eldrin, and his eyes were the grey of the summit's sky, and they were warm. "That's why I'm on this road, Eldrin. Not for the finding. For the *believing* — that there's still a record somewhere that wasn't erased, that the tidying didn't get everything, that a man can walk east his whole life and never run out of road."

Eldrin looked at him across the fire, at the man who had read the snow and split the firewood and carried the heavier pack and fought a ghost wearing his own face, and the splinter shifted in him, and he let it shift, and he did not reach for it.

"Maybe it wasn't a library," he said. "Maybe it was the record of the world itself. Maybe that's what a thousand sheep carry, and what a man walks east for — not a collection, the *keeping* of it. The proof that something was carried, that something held." He stopped, because the sentence had gone further than he meant it to, and the firelight was very warm, and the lie he had told on the descent was very tired.

Oren looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved. The fire moved.

"That's the truest thing you've said all day," Oren said, gently. "And I'm going to remember it." He banked the fire, a small domestic gesture, the gesture that had opened the door to the question at the foot of the mountains — the question about the end of the road, the one that had arrived perfectly aimed. But he did not ask it again. He let the fire go down. He spread his bedroll and lay down in the warmth of the fold, and he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said, into the dark, "Whatever the whisperer showed you, Eldrin — I'd be a poorer chronicler than I am if I didn't know that the mountain takes things from a man and gives them back different. You don't have to tell me. You just have to walk east with me in the morning."

The words sat in the firelight between them, and the trust — the thing that had been rebuilt every night around the fire, the thing Malphas had tried to take apart, the thing Eldrin had been hiding behind a lie for five days — stood up in Eldrin's chest and took a breath, and the breath was almost whole.

"I'll walk east," he said. "It's the only direction I have left."

They slept. The fire went down to ash. The mountain's white went pale in the east. And in the small hours, with the fold full of the quiet breathing of a sleeping man who was not, as far as anyone could prove, a danger, Eldrin lay awake with his eyes closed and the needle in his skull and the splinter in his side, and he let the story of the thousand sheep carry the weight of the night.

The trust was whole. The trust was nearly whole. The crack was closed, or nearly closed, and in the firelight, in the warmth, in the ordinary breathing of the dark, it was almost possible to believe that the crack had never been there at all.

That was the deceptive calm, and the deception, for one night, worked.
