# Ch. 6 — The Ascent
<!-- Track: 4. Summit of Despair | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late winter -->

The pass took the air first. That was the thing no map had warned him of — the maps of Thaloria were honest about the shape of mountains and entirely silent about the price of the climb, and the price was a slow, patient thinning of the world until every breath came due with interest. Eldrin had walked east out of a city that had never once asked his lungs for more than a staircase, and the pass asked him for everything at once, in the small hours, when the wind had learned his name and was teaching it to the stone.

Oren carried the heavier pack. That was the first proof of the man's usefulness, and it was not the last. Eldrin had offered to trade at the foot of the pass, and Oren had declined with the particular good nature of a man who knows his own strength and is not interested in performing it. He read the snow the way Eldrin read stone — by the settled habits of a thing that had been telling the truth for a long time — and he had found them shelter each night without ever making the finding look like work: a wind-scoured hollow, a ledge with a southern face, a fallen trunk that had been leaning against the weather for a hundred years and had kept its promise. He broke the trail in the deep drifts without being asked. He divided the firewood and the dried meat and the last of the good tea into even shares and pretended he was not doing it. And when Eldrin's old hip began its quiet protest against the grade, Oren said nothing at all, which was, Eldrin would learn, the man's way of kindness — to take the weight and not make a moment of it.

The needle in his skull had begun to tremble on the second day of the climb.

It had trembled at the foot of the mountains, a fine continuous vibration like a wire struck somewhere along its length. Now it trembled with a steadier, deeper urgency, the way a needle trembles when the thing it points at has become close enough to be felt rather than merely known. Eldrin said nothing about it. He had learned that the compass was not a thing to discuss — it was a private ledger, kept against the day the world would ask him to justify his walking — and so he carried it in silence, and the trembling sat under his thoughts like a second pulse, and Oren, walking a stride ahead with the wind at his back, did not ask why an old scholar kept stopping to look east.

They reached the summit on the fourth day, and the summit was exactly what the old tales had always insisted it would be. *Ice-kissed peaks, a barren expanse.* The pass widened into a high plateau of wind-carved snow, a great bare shoulder of the mountain where nothing grew and nothing moved and the sky lay on the world like a sheet of cold glass. The wind had a sound there that Eldrin had heard only once before, in the shepherd's lean-to at the foot of the mountains — the thin continuous note of a blade drawn and not yet used — and it never stopped. The ice did not glitter; it glared, a white that was also a kind of blindness, a color that had forgotten every other color. He stood on the summit of the world's oldest road and the summit gave him back nothing but the wind and the white and the small cramped sound of his own breathing.

And it was there, in the middle of that barren expanse, that the doubt found him.

It came the way all his doubts came — quietly, dressed in the voice of reason, carrying its evidence in order. *You are sixty years old. You are a historian, and you have spent the winter being carried east by a compass you cannot show anyone, toward a stone no living man has seen, on the orders of a storm you cannot prove exists. The woman in the vision was a dream; the broken man was a dream; the violet sky was a dream that has kept you walking through a country where the dreams of old men are generally the least reliable thing in it. And the man behind you — the one who splits your firewood and reads your snow for you — he is kind to you, and kindness is the one currency you have never learned to spend carefully.* The doubt did not shout. It did not need to shout. It had the whole of the empty summit for a pulpit, and the wind for a congregation, and the note the blade sang was the only sound in the world that answered it.

He stood there longer than he should have. Oren had stopped a few strides on, waiting, his back to the wind, his breath smoking in the white — patient, unquestioning, a man who had learned that a scholar's pauses were not to be hurried. And Eldrin looked at him and thought: *I do not know this man. I have been walking with him for two weeks and I know the names of villages he has never visited and the handwriting of a minor chronicler who amuses him, and I do not know the first true thing about him, and I have let him carry the heavier pack.*

He filed the thought. That was the discipline — to file it, to date it, to set it beside the other doubts and let them argue it out among themselves. He had argued with doubt for sixty years and won by refusing to let it be the last word. He would not let the summit have the last word either.

And yet the argument would not close, and he knew the reason for it, because he was a scholar and he had named the reason the way he named everything. The men who asked about the Anchor — the travelers of the trader's story, the questioners of a hundred villages — moved like scholars with a single question in their sleeves. They were quiet, and they were methodical, and they were searching. Whatever had come down this pass was not searching. It had passed through a camp of fourteen and left it a frozen ledger, and it had not stopped to ask anything, because it already knew what it wanted and where the wanting pointed. The needle trembled in his skull, and the trembling was not the trembling of a thing being approached. It was the trembling of a thing being *shared* — the same fixed certainty, the same direction, held in another hand somewhere ahead of him in the white.

*You are not the only compass,* he thought, and the thought went down into him like a cold wire.

Oren's voice came to him across the plateau, and he turned, and the doubt had to stand aside for the ordinary business of the day, because the summit would not wait and neither would the man who had split his firewood every night for two weeks. He answered, and they went on, and the doubt went with them, filed, dated, waiting to be argued again.

It was Oren who saw the camp.

They had crossed half the plateau, heads down against the wind, when Oren's hand came up — a small precise gesture, the hand of a man who has read a thousand trails and just found the one sentence that did not belong. Eldrin stopped. The wind had dropped a little, or the ear had gone numb to it, and in the new silence there was a shape ahead: a low line of stones and a sagging ridgepole, half-buried in drift, the geometry of human work under a country that had no use for it.

"Camp," Oren said. He did not move toward it. His voice had gone flat, the way it went when he was reading something he did not want to read. "A big one. And it's been there through the storm."

They approached it the way men approach anything that has been still too long in a bad country — slowly, with the wind at their backs and their hands free. Eldrin saw the first body before they reached the stones. It was kneeling, the way a man kneels to a fire, and it was frozen so completely that the cold had preserved the exact posture of its last intention: one hand reaching, the other braced against the snow, the face turned toward the camp's center as if the thing it had wanted were still there, still burning, still worth the reaching. There was no wound on it that Eldrin could see. There was no color in it at all, nothing but the pale wax of a man the cold had taken without a struggle, and that was the wrongness of it, the first of many. A man who freezes fights the freezing. This one had gone down in the middle of the act of living, mid-motion, mid-reach, as if the cold had not crept up on him but had simply been *placed* on him, all at once, from above.

The rest of the camp bore it out. A dozen bodies, or fifteen — it was hard to count, and counting was the kind of thing Eldrin did, so he counted, and the count came to fourteen. They lay and knelt and sat in the attitudes of a camp taken at its ordinary hour: a man mending harness, a woman at a cookfire that had never finished its work, two figures at a table with a map between them, their hands still resting on the edges of it as if the negotiation had been interrupted and would be resumed the moment the storm lifted. There were no weapons drawn. There were no wounds. There were no tracks leading out of the camp — the drifts had covered every direction with an equal and impartial hand — and there was no blood, and there was no sign, anywhere in that frozen room of a world, of the thing that had come among them.

And there was the beast.

It lay at the edge of the camp, the largest thing in the white, and it was not a thing Eldrin had a name for. A Crag-Fiend — the old hunters' word surfaced out of the bestiary in the Archive's less-tidied corners — a mountain predator as long as a sled and as grey as the rock it had come from, a thing built of shoulders and patience that fed on the sheep of the high folds and the occasional shepherd. It was dead. It had been killed the same way the camp had been killed, without a struggle and without a wound, frozen in the middle of its own last step, and its hide, along the spine and the flanks, was grown over in places with crystals.

Violet crystals.

They grew out of the grey fur like the ice-flowers that form on a window in a hard winter, but they were not frost and they were not pale. They were the color of the underside of the sky in his vision — the bruise and the wound at once — and they caught the flat white light and threw it back as something else, something that had no right to the light, and they were beautiful in the way a socket is beautiful, which is to say they were the sign of a vacancy that had been filled with the wrong thing.

Eldrin knelt by the beast and looked at the crystals for a long time. He was a scholar; he did not need to be told what he was looking at. He had spent his life reading the seams of the world, and he had learned to recognize the place where the world's hand had been lifted from the page. The crystals were such a place. They were the corruption of the deep places, the leech-thing he had seen in the eyes of the shadow-touched wolves — grown, now, out of the open, wearing the color of the vision's sky like a flag. And the camp, the fourteen frozen, the beast that had died mid-step with no wound — they were not the work of the cold. The cold had been the *instrument*. Someone had come down this pass with the winter in his hand, and he had laid it on the camp the way a man lays a cloth over a cage, and he had gone on.

"Eldrin."

Oren's voice came from the far side of the camp, low and level. Eldrin rose and went to him. Oren was standing at the edge of the stones with the wind at his face, looking at something in the snow beyond the camp — a set of tracks, clean and separate, that ran out of the white from the direction of the summit and crossed the camp's margin without slowing. They were not the tracks of the frozen. They were the tracks of a single man, and they had been made after the killing, because they crossed the snow above the bodies without breaking any of them, and they continued east, into the pass, into the country the maps did not cover.

"A single set," Oren said. He was not asking. His face had the careful blankness of a man filing something large. "Fourteen dead, a beast with crystals growing out of it, and one set of tracks walking out the far side. No one else went in. No one else came out." He looked at Eldrin, and there was something in his grey eyes that Eldrin had not seen there before — a seriousness, a weight, the look of a man who has just discovered that the road he chose to walk is not the road he was told about. "You knew there was someone ahead of us. You've known since the village with the trader. Whoever this was, he came down off this mountain like it was a staircase, and he left the winter behind him to do his work. That's not a man who's ahead of you, Eldrin. That's a man who's *waiting*."

The wind took the sentence and scattered it. Eldrin stood at the edge of the frozen camp with the needle trembling in his skull and the violet crystals burning at the corner of his sight, and he knew he should say something. He knew the moment called for the truth, or the nearest licensed relative of the truth — that he had been sent east by a storm, that the storm had given him a compass no one could see, that the men who asked about the Anchor were not the only things hunting in this white country. He opened his mouth to say it, and the summit's silence closed over him instead, and what came out was the smaller, safer thing.

"We go on," he said. "Whoever he is, he's ahead of us, and the road we need is the road he's already taken. We'll read his tracks the way you read the snow, and we'll keep our distance, and we'll reach the end of this pass before he reaches whatever he's going to."

Oren looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved between them. Then the man's face did the thing Eldrin had come to recognize — the small interior shift from weighing to decided — and he nodded, and he turned back toward the camp, and he began, methodically, to check the frozen dead for anything worth the carrying. He did it without ceremony, the way a man does any necessary thing, and Eldrin watched him and felt the weight of the doubt shift a little, like cargo settling.

"Eldrin," Oren said, without looking up. He was crouched by the frozen woman at the dead cookfire, and his voice had gone quiet, almost gentle. "I want you to know. I came on this road because I wanted the books — the lost libraries, the records of the kings who ruled before the Gap. That's true. I've told you nothing that isn't true, and I'll tell you nothing that isn't, because a chronicler who lies is a man building his own erasure." He straightened, and he looked at Eldrin across the frozen camp, and his eyes were the grey of the summit's sky, and there was a steadiness in them that had nothing to do with the cold. "But I didn't stay on this road for the books. I stayed because you're the first man I've met in twenty years who talks about the Gap the way I do — like it's a wound and not a fact. Whatever's at the end of this pass, and whatever's waiting in it, I'll walk it with you. That's a promise, and I don't break promises."

It was the right thing to say. It was, Eldrin thought, exactly the right thing to say, and he said so with his eyes, and the doubt settled a little more, and the two of them stood in the frozen camp of the fourteen dead with the wind singing its thin note over the summit and the violet crystals burning at the edge of the world.

They buried what they could. It took the rest of the day. Oren did the digging, and Eldrin did the counting — the ledger of the dead, the small service of naming what the cold had taken — and when the light failed and the wind rose, they walked on, east, out of the plateau and into the descending pass, with the tracks of the single man running before them like a sentence already written.

The needle trembled. The tracks went east. And somewhere ahead of them, in the white country the maps did not cover, the man who had brought the winter walked on, waiting for the piece the storm had moved.
