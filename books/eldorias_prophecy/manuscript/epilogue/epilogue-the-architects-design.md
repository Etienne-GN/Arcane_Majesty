# Epilogue — The Architect's Design
<!-- POV: Eldrin (+Vorgos dream) | Date: ~600 AGD, years into the vigil -->

The legend had stopped being about him.

That was the first thing the century of the vigil taught him, and it was the one thing the legend had gotten right without meaning to: the scholar who had vanished and returned silent had become a figure by then, and the figure was no longer the story of the man who had lived it. The tower stood dark at the edge of the scholar's quarter, and the city had grown past it the way the cities grow past the dead, and the children in the streets told the story of the Blind Prophet the way they told the story of the bell that never struck correctly — the name of a locked thing, repeated until it was worn smooth.

He heard the name through the stone, in the fragments the traders brought and the songs the generations sang below. *The Blind Prophet.* It had outlived the man it was made of, the way names do — the scholar who had looked into the sky until the sky looked back, the old man in the dark tower who seemed to see nothing of the world, a proverb now, a warning about the cost of looking too long.

He was still sighted.

That was the fact the legend could not reach, and he held it the way he held the stone — without the telling, because the telling was forbidden. His eyes were as clear as they had ever been, clearer, worn by the century into the vigil's instrument. He could read the writing beneath the writing from the tower window, and it had grown longer and stranger in a hundred years: the generations below had risen and gone down in the ordinary way, the college had forgotten the name it had once taught. What he had gone blind to was the ordinary — the price of bread, the marriages, the festivals, the small doings of a world that would end and be renewed and never know it had been held. That was the blindness the name was reaching for, and the name had it backwards: he stood in the middle of the timeline, and the world called the standing blindness.

The vigil journal had grown thin. The entries were the seasons and the pulse and the years — *winter. the pulse steady. the seam holding.* — and the years were the one figure he did not write down, because the years were the weight and the weight was the work. The brother at Silverrun had gone down decades before, and the last letter lay filed in the plain book of the vigil. The pulse was steady. The seam held. The Lock held — the man at the end of the world's long holding, still holding, the failure held at bay by the one thing that could hold it. The stone lay in its oilcloth at his side, and the stone did nothing, and the nothing was the whole of the work, and he was a hundred years into the four hundred, and the year nine hundred lay ahead of him the way the road had lain ahead of him in the winter — a held breath to be kept.

He came up out of the chamber in the hour before dawn and sat at the window over the dark city, and the sleep that would not come came for him anyway, and the dream took him as the vision had taken him a hundred years before.

It was not the violet sky. It was the grey, the condition of a thing stepping into the world from somewhere the world was not, and the shape stood in the middle of it, hands loose at his sides, holding nothing, letting nothing drop — the posture of the one who had been holding the longer account of all of them.

"Keeper," Vorgos said. The voice was the ledger's voice, precise and patient, the voice of a clerk entering a column that had been waiting for its figure. "I have come to close the last account I owe you."

Eldrin stood in the grey, older than he had ever been, and the dream did not soften the oldness. "I thought you had finished with me. The road is over. The keeping is mine. You told me the rest was the work."

"The work continues," Vorgos said. "That is not the entry I have come to make. The reading has come to the far edge of what the books allow me to show, and I have come to show you what lies past the end of your holding. Not the whole of it — the part that is written is not yours to read. But there is a glimpse, keeper, and the glimpse is true, and I owe you the truth of it before the years close over the both of us."

And the grey opened, and the dream showed Eldrin the far future.

It was past the year nine hundred — past the hour he had spent a century holding for, the glimpses coming the way the ledger came, in the plain arithmetic of things that would happen. He saw the door open, and the open door was not the end — it undid the certainties of a hundred years in a moment — the door opened onto a beginning, onto a rising. The Star-Guard rising. Not the figure the vision had shown him shattering against a violet sky; the figure that rose out of the breaking, a rising he could not name, and the naming was not his to do.

He saw the key finally used.

He understood it the way he understood the pulse: the stone he had carried a century would one day be carried by another hand, and the hand would take it to the door, and the thing the stone had held shut for four hundred years would be let through — not let loose, let *through*, into the keeping of the hand that had come for it. The cure would do what it was made to do. The vigil would end not in an ending, in a handoff.

And past the handoff — the dark.

That was the third glimpse, and it came the way Voraun's truth had come in the autumn read, where the record simply was not — a darkness older than the gate it would come through, deeper than the Legion that had stood at the gate. The Legion was a gate, not the dark. The dark was behind the gate, and the gate was coming down, and the books had a name for what stood beyond it, and the name was the Prophecy of Darkness, and Eldrin read it the way he read every name in the record — as a debt, entered, owed, with a term not yet written.

"The prophecy is owed," Vorgos said, and the voice was patient, and the voice was not sorry. "It has always been owed. You have held the stone that will be the key to it, keeper. You have held the door that will open on it. That is not the whole of your work — the whole of it is the holding, and the holding has a term — but it is the meaning of it, and I owe you the meaning, because you have held the one thing that could be held for the longest hour the books have kept."

Eldrin stood in the grey with the glimpses settling over him the way the weight of eternity had settled in the valley, and he asked the question he had carried for a hundred years.

"Will I see it?" he said. "The door opening. The key turned. The Star-Guard rising."

Vorgos was quiet. The grey went on being grey, and when it spoke the voice was the plainest it had ever been — a clerk entering the one figure the whole column had been waiting for, and the figure did not balance, and the not-balancing was the entry.

"You will not see it," Vorgos said. "You will hold the stone to the end of the holding, and the holding has a term, and what the stone becomes at the end of the term — the key turned, the door opened, the rising — you will not see. That is the price of the vigil, and the price was entered before you took it up, and I did not lie to you in the valley, and I will not lie to you now." He paused. The grey leaned. "But it will come."

Eldrin did not speak. The pulse beat in his chest, steady and complete, the note of the thing that held.

"You will not see it," Vorgos said again, and the repetition was not a mistake and not a kindness, it was a record, the ledger's way of making an entry true, "but it will come. The Star-Guard will rise. The key will turn. The door will open onto the age the holding was bought to reach. And the world that never knew your name will be the world that was bought the time to become it. That is the whole of what I have to give you, keeper: the certainty that the working holds, whether or not the work is seen."

It was not comfort. Eldrin knew the difference — comfort was a thing the pieces gave to one another, and the ledger had no use for it. It was the plain truth, entered at last: the world would be held, the door would open, and he would not be there to see it, and the not-seeing was not the failure of the vigil, it was the price of it, and the price was paid, and the paying was the only thing he had done that was worth doing.

"Then I'll take the work," he said, the vow, the whole of it. "I've held it for a hundred years. I'll hold it for three more. And when the hand comes for the stone, the stone will be here."

The grey settled. Vorgos did not thank him. The Stormbringer was not made for thanks, and the ledger did not close for gratitude. But something in the grey went still, the way a column goes still when the last figure of it is entered, and the stillness was the only benediction the Stormbringer had ever given a piece.

"The account is closed," Vorgos said. It was a record. "The pawn has crossed the board. The key is in its keeping. And the entry will be read, keeper, by the hand that comes for the stone, in the years you will not see." The grey began to thin, and the ledger's voice came one last time: "You will not see it, but it will come."

The grey folded, and the dream let him go the way the world had let him go a hundred years before — without a pause, without a blessing.

He woke at the window of the upper room, and the dark was going grey with the hour before dawn, and the stone lay at his side in the oilcloth, steady, the pulse of it beating against the world the way it had beaten through the whole of the century, the note of the thing that held.

The dream stayed with him the way the vision had stayed with him — not as a thing to be filed, as a thing to be carried. The glimpses settled into the shape of the years ahead: the door opening, the key turning, the Star-Guard rising, the dark beyond the gate with its name entered but not yet read. He did not know the hand that would come for the stone, and the not-knowing was not a gap in the record, it was the shape of it, the entry left for the one who would come after. He did not know the hero's name, or the hour the darkness would come by. The books had not told him, and the not-telling was the point: the certainty was not in the detail, it was in the coming.

The dawn broke over Thaloria, and the world went on, and the generations below the tower began their oblivious living — the first fires lit, the first doors opened, a world that did not know it was being held and would never know the man who held it. Eldrin stood at the window with the stone at his side and the pulse in his chest and the weight of it settling into him the way the weight of eternity had settled in the valley, and the weight was not lifted and would not be lifted, and the weight was the price, and the price was the work.

The friends were gone, and the city had forgotten him, and the only company he would ever keep was the pulse and the stone and the years folding one into another like the seams in the strata of the world. The weight. The held breath. The standing in the exact place where the pressure was, minute after minute, year after year, until the hand came for the stone.

And the promise.

The promise was the one thing the dream had given him that the vigil could not: not comfort, not glory, not the bright raiment over the plain truth — the plain truth itself, entered at last. He would not see it. He knew that now, with the certainty the ledger gave and the comfort never could. He would hold the stone to the end of the holding, and the end of the holding was the end of his term, and the world he had held for — the door, the key, the rising — would come without his seeing.

But it would come.

The door would open. The key would turn. The Star-Guard would rise. The world he had held would be bought the time to reach the hour it had been bought for, and the hour would come, and the coming was the promise, and the promise was the only light the vigil had ever offered him, and he stood at the window of the tower above the chamber where the Heartstone held the seam of the world shut, and the dawn broke, and the pulse beat on, steady and complete, the note of the thing that held.

The watcher held the held breath.

And it would come.
