# Ch. 2 — The Vision
<!-- Track: 2. Dreamweaver's Call | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, early winter -->

He was floating. That was the first thing his body reported, and his body was a scholar: it demanded its evidence in order. The floor of the tower had gone, but so had the floor of everything else, and what held him now was nothing a foundation could be made of. His feet were gone. He had the *memory* of feet, and the memory was doing its best, and it was failing, the way a musician fails a tune he has not played in thirty years.

Time had been pulled out of him. He knew that later, and the knowing came too late to help. At the moment it happened he felt only the seamlessness of the thing — how gently a century can be drawn from a man, like a thread from a loom, and how the cloth does not notice. The loom of his life went on weaving without him. The night went on being early winter. And Eldrin Nightshade went on, elsewhere.

The elsewhere had a sky. That was his first true datum, the one his mind caught and held against the drift: a sky, and the sky was the color of a bruise and a wound at once, only now he was *inside* the bruise, standing under the wound as a man stands under weather. Violet poured down in sheets without weight, light falling across a world that had never once heard of light. He tried to make a map of it, because that was what he did with any new country — he located the edges, he found the features, he named the rivers of it — and the map came apart in his hands. Nothing held its distance. Distance itself had a pulse. The ground underfoot was memory, not substance, stretched thin as candle-wax over something that breathed underneath.

He was looking at the future. Not a possible future, not a painted one — the actual one, wearing itself out in front of him like a fire nobody had thought to bank.

The city lay wrong around him. Spires he almost recognized, built on foundations that had shifted a finger's width out of true, as if the world had set its bones badly and gone on walking. Towers that should have been stone were grown over in the color of the sky, and the light they gave off was the light of something that had stopped being matter. And at the far edge of the wrong city, where the horizon had gone thin as a held breath, there was a figure.

He did not see it clearly. He saw what it was *doing*, which was holding. A man — he knew it was a man, the way you know a signature without being able to read the hand — standing against the sky with his arms spread as if he were holding a door without a frame, a gate without a post, a seal with no name. Light pooled under him and around him and through him. He was the last thing standing between the violet and everything else, and he was doing it the way a dam holds a river: by being exactly where the pressure was, minute after minute, year after year, until the water learned his name and wore him smooth.

Eldrin had spent his life reading the world's bones. He recognized the posture. It was the posture of a man carrying a wound that had been stitched over and called healed — the posture of the Silent Century itself, standing on a seam.

And then the man's hands slipped.

Not violently. That was the terrible part. The holding gave way the way a rope gives, strand by strand, each surrender a quiet matter of record. Light came out of the man in shards — not flung, not spilled, *shattered*, the way a bell shatters when the note goes wrong. And Eldrin felt it. That was the thing he would never be able to explain, in any journal he would ever keep: the resonance of it. The breaking man sang as he broke, a single note, sustained, exact, and the note went through Eldrin's chest like a cold wire, and he understood, without a single word having passed, that this was what the end of the world would sound like when it finally tuned itself.

A killing sound.

He said it later, in his own head, in the voice he used for heresy: *a killing sound*. A sound that did not stop the heart so much as rename it.

The man fell. The shards of him went out across the violet like a coin dropped in dark water, each one striking its own small, exact note, each note a claim on something that could no longer be claimed. And where the man had stood, holding the door that had no frame, there was nothing. There was *through*. The violet poured into the gap the way water pours into a foundered ship — unhurriedly, at first. As if it had all the time in the world. As if it had been waiting four hundred years and could wait four hundred more.

A woman ran to the broken man.

Eldrin had not seen her until she moved. She must have been there all along — kneeling, perhaps, or standing guard at his feet the way a soldier stands guard at a wall — but she had been so still, so much a part of the wrong city's furniture, that his eye had crossed over her. She ran, and the running undid her stillness, and she fell on her knees beside the man who had held the door, and she gathered his head in her lap, and she wept.

It was not a noise. That was what he would remember. The weeping made no noise at all; it was too far gone for noise, or the noise had been taken from it, or — and this was the thought that stayed — she had been weeping for so long that the sound had been worn out of her, the way a river wears out its own bed. She held the broken man, and her shoulders did the thing shoulders do when grief has emptied them of everything but the motion, and Eldrin stood at the edge of the world and watched a woman mourn the end of the world and could not reach her.

He tried. He had to record that, in the ledger of his own shame: he tried to move toward her, and his feet — his *remembered* feet — refused to answer, because there was nothing under them to walk on. He was a reader at the edge of a manuscript, and the manuscript was burning, and he could sooner step into it than unsay a sentence.

She did not look up. That was the detail he could not shake, afterwards, the one that would come back to him in the grey hours: the woman never once raised her eyes. She searched for no rescue, sought no cause for the fall, and held the broken man to her chest. Her grief was silence — a *vigil*. The patience of a thing that had decided long ago to be the one to remain, and that remaining was its work, and that work would outlast the world. She would sit there holding him, Eldrin understood, until the violet took her — and she knew it, had known it for a long time, and knowing had never once made her look up.

He had seen the posture before. It was the posture of the keepers, the watchers, the ones who stand at a door because someone must. It was the posture he had spent his life envying without knowing he was envying it. And it was the posture, he understood now, that the storm had brought him here to witness — not the shattering, not the falling, not the wound itself, but the *watching*. The grief that would not be rescued. The woman who held.

The weeping went on. The violet poured. The shards of the broken man lay where they had fallen, each one still faintly singing its own small killing note, and Eldrin understood that he was being shown something — not told, *shown*, the way the masters showed a novice a seal by pressing it into his palm — and that the showing was not for his benefit. It was for his *education*. Someone wanted him to have this image intact, warm, unhealable, so that he would carry it.

So that he would go.

"Eldrin."

The voice came from the sky. No — the voice came from the *condition* of the sky, from the bruise and the wound and the pouring violet, from the very wrongness he had been standing in, and it wore the sky the way a man wears a coat. He looked up, and the sky looked back.

It had an eye.

Not in the way of a face — the sky had no countenance, which would have been a comfort, a familiar shape for terror to put on. It had an eye the way a storm has a center: a place where all the turning organized itself, a deep violet-veined pupil of almost-black, ringed in the color of old blood, and it was fixed on him with the attention of something that had seen him a long time ago and had been patient ever since. The rest of the sky was the storm around it — banks and shelves and rolling accumulations of weather without temperature, cloud that held no water, wind that moved no air. The eye held him. The storm considered him.

He knew the name before it was spoken, the way you know a signature without reading the hand. *Vorgos.* The Stormbringer. The wind that had moved through the empty shelves of the Forbidden Archive. The one thing in the Gap that was not missing.

"The Lock will fail."

The words came from everywhere at once, from the fabric of the vision itself — a *finding*, not a warning. A warning is information offered in good faith, so that the warned may choose. This was a verdict already entered, a clerk reading the charge back to the prisoner. The Lock will fail. It was not a question of whether. It was a question of when, and the when had a schedule, and the schedule was already in arrears.

"Find the Anchor."

He had heard that word before. The letter. The letter from the monastery at Silverrun, ten days old now — or a hundred years old, or four hundred years old, time being what it was in this place — telling him about the travelers who passed through every village and shrine and inn along the river, asking one question, never resting, never quarreling, never explaining. *The Anchor. What it was. Where it lay. Who kept it.*

And now the sky was telling him the same word, and he understood, with the clarity that comes only in dreams and at the very end of things, that he had been standing on the far side of the question his whole life. He had been the man who could not find the crack in the glass. He had spent forty years mapping the seams of a wound. And the wound had a name after all — not a phrase, not a hypothesis, a *name*, and the name had been given to the sky, and the sky had been waiting for him to ask.

"The Anchor is the Heartstone of Creation. It is what holds. It has always been what holds. Find it, and it will hold again."

The eye did not soften. It offered no encouragement, no comfort, no small mercies a man negotiating with a god might hope to receive. It was, he would realize much later, the first honest thing that had spoken to him in forty years: it wanted something from him, told him exactly what it wanted, and made no pretense that wanting was the same as deserving. Vorgos the Stormbringer was not asking Eldrin Nightshade to save the world. He was informing him that the world had been saved before, by methods that had failed, and that the methods must be renewed, and that Eldrin was the renewing.

It was not a request. It was the Aether itself, the thin strained music of the world, reaching out through its storm the way a drowning man reaches for anything that floats, and using the only thing it had to hold on to.

"Why me?"

He asked it aloud. He was uncertain about his mouth, but the question came out anyway, a small human thing in the middle of the violet, and he expected no answer, and he got none. The eye did not blink — a storm never blinks — but something in its attention shifted, like a reader turning a page past a passage irrelevant to the argument. The question had been weighed. The question had been found to have no purchase. There was no reason, or the reason belonged to someone else, or the reason was so simple it would die in the saying. He was here. He was available. He was the piece on the board that could be moved.

He tried the other questions, because a man who has spent his life in archives will always try the other questions, and he was that man even here, even now, standing under a violet sky with a storm for a judge. Who was the woman? Who was the man who had held the door? What was the Lock he had held, and who had made it, and what did it mean that it was failing now, after so long, instead of last year or in a hundred years' time? He asked them all, and he asked them the way he had asked the strata — carefully, politely, one at a time, as if each answer were a thing that must be signed for.

The eye answered none of them.

That was the moment he understood the shape of the thing he was dealing with. The storm withheld — not out of malice, mystery, or the love of riddles the old tales gave their gods — but because it was *efficient*. It had one thing to give him, and it had given it, and the rest of the sky was done with conversation. Vorgos was a sentinel, a signal-fire, a courier who had been carrying this message for four hundred years and had found the one man awake to receive it. He would say what was necessary, and the necessary was finished, and the rest of the hour belonged to the message.

The eye turned from him — the attention moving off him like a wind moving off a field — and the vision began, at last, to be about him in a different way.

"Rise, Eldrin. The century is late."

The words arrived, and with them the *compass*.

He felt it as a burning. Not a metaphor — an actual, layered, ongoing burning, as if a needle had been drawn across the inside of his skull in a line from the base of his neck to a point behind his right eye, and the line had been left there, molten, cooling into something that would never cool again. He cried out. He was fairly certain he cried out; there was no air in this place to carry the sound, but his body tried, and the body's trying was a fact, and the fact was his.

And when the burning settled — when it finished arranging itself into a shape his mind could hold — he understood what it had left behind. An orientation. A direction. A knowledge, as immediate as the knowledge of which way is down, that there was a place in the world where something *was*, and that the something was east, and that the distance to it was a fixed and constant ache, like a compass whose needle has been nailed to the page and which has given up its freedom in exchange for a certainty.

It pointed to the Heartstone. He knew that without being told, the way he knew the name of the sky. It would always point to the Heartstone. He could go where he liked, live how he liked, refuse every command of every god, and the needle would not care. It would lie in his mind, patient as a tide, pointing east, to the ruins, to the thing that held.

The eye was gone. The storm was gone. The weeping woman and the broken man were gone — but no, not gone, *filed*; he would find them again in the dark behind his eyes every time he closed them for the rest of his life, the way the masters had promised the novice he would find the seal. The violet sky was gone, draining out of the world the way the tide drains out of a wound, and the room was coming back.

The room. The tower. The candle.

He was lying on the floor.

The fall had happened at some point — he did not know when; the floor had been under him when the vision released him, and the floor had caught him, and the stone had been cold, and he had lain there long enough for the cold to enter him. The candle was burning. The candle had been relit, or had relit itself, or had simply endured; the small domestic flame stood upright in its pool of tallow and burned with the serene idiocy of all small domestic things. The bell had stopped. The room had returned to being a room — the strata cores in their slots, the journals on their shelves, the violet ink on the map of the world grown dark again, just ink, heresy, an old man's marginal note.

He lay on the stones of his own floor and breathed, and the breathing was a miracle, and he did not feel miraculous. He felt hollowed. He felt the way a bell must feel in the instant after it has been struck, before the note has had time to decide whether it was a note or a crack.

And beneath the hollow, deep and low and steady as a second heartbeat, the needle lay in his mind.

East.

He turned his head, slowly, on the cold stone, as if he could see through the walls of the tower and the hills of Thaloria and the whole breadth of the Silent Century to the mountains where the map stopped. The needle stayed fixed. The needle had given up its freedom in exchange for a certainty, and the certainty had been placed in him by a storm, and the storm had called him by name, and the name had been spoken by something that did not need him.

And yet — and this was the thought that finally, gently, undid the last of his composure — *the storm had called him by name.* Not by his title. Not by his function. Not by his position on the board, though that was all he was to it. By his name. The way you call a thing you have been waiting for. The way you call a thing you mean to keep.

Someone had been listening. And listening, it turned out, had been only the beginning.
