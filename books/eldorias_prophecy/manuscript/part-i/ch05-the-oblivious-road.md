# Ch. 5 — The Oblivious Road
<!-- Track: 3. Odyssey's Dawn | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, late winter -->

The road taught him its rhythm in the first week, and the rhythm was mostly waiting. Waiting for the frost to break under his boots. Waiting for the next inn to come around the bend with its plume of smoke and its smell of soup. Waiting for dusk, which came early and stayed late, and which he spent learning the grammar of small fires: how to build them so the light did not reach, how to choose a hollow that would not funnel the smoke, how to bank the warmth so that the dark found nothing to attend to. He had spent sixty years believing that being careful meant being right. The road revised the definition. Being careful meant being *unseen*.

He traveled as a wanderer-scholar. No title, no city, no college. When asked — and the road asked, in the way of all roads — he was a student of the old stones and the older roads, walking east to look at ruins that no one else remembered. It was true enough, and it was the truth that fit in a sentence, and the sentence was the difference between being filed and being remembered. The innkeepers filed him. The villagers filed him. The children, who had the unsettling habit of assessment, filed him and then lost interest, because an old man with a satchel who walked east to look at ruins was exactly the kind of ordinary the world could forget.

He was grateful for the forgetting. He was also, by the second week, lonely in a way that surprised him. He had lived alone for thirty years and had never once been lonely; the tower had been full of company — the cores, the journals, the margins gnawed with questions, the small specific conversations a man has with his own evidence. The road had none of that. The road had walking, and the needle, and the terrible blue sky, and the knowledge, growing sharper every day, that he was the only man in the world carrying this particular record. It was not that he wanted to tell anyone about the vision. It was that he wanted, once, to hear a voice that knew what it was talking about.

The rumors found him at the inns. They were the road's true currency, passing from table to table with the salt, and he sat in corners and listened the way a scholar listens to any source: weighing, comparing, discounting the obvious embroidery. A drover swore he had seen a violet afterglow in the eastern sky last spring, a thing like a bruise on the underside of the night, and the sky had gone on being ordinary, and no one believed him. A farmer had lost a dog to the eastern wood, and the dog had been found with its eyes burned out, and the farmer had not gone back for the cart. A trader from Port Silver told of a stranger who had come through the winter market asking, in a soft unhurried voice, about the roads east — *the old roads, the ones that go to the mountains* — and whether they were passable, and who lived along them, and whether anyone in the villages spoke of a thing called the Anchor.

*The Anchor.*

Eldrin's hand went still on his cup. The trader, who had not noticed, went on with his story: the stranger had paid well and asked no second question, and the trader had not been able to say why the encounter had stayed with him, except that the stranger had asked the way a man asks when he means to use the road and does not intend to share it.

Eldrin listened. And because he was a scholar, he did not merely listen — he *dated* it. The trader's stranger was not the same man as the brother's travelers, or the travelers of the villages west of Thaloria; the road was long, and the men who asked one question in every village did not stay to ask it twice in the same place. They moved in a line, eastward, quietly, and they asked about the Anchor, and they were somewhere on this road, ahead of him or behind him or both.

The race was real.

He had known it, in the way a man knows a letter is important before he opens it. But knowing and *knowing* are different things, and the trader's story turned the knowing into something he could weigh. There were others seeking the Anchor. They were not scholars — scholars quarreled, scholars kept their questions in their sleeves and drew them out one at a time, like cards they were not sure they wanted to play. These men moved in a line, asking one question, never resting, never quarreling, never explaining. They were searching for the same thing the needle pointed at, and they were searching quietly, and quiet searching meant they did not want to be found, and what did not want to be found was not here to ask permission.

He did not know who they were. He did not know what they wanted with the Anchor, or whether they knew what the Anchor was, or whether they would know it when they found it. He knew only that he had been given the needle, and the needle was not a gift — it was a *task*, and the task had a schedule, and the schedule was in arrears.

He went east a little faster after that. And he kept his eyes on the road ahead, and on the road behind, and on the small dark spaces between the trees where a quiet man might stand and ask his one question and move on.

The day he met Oren, the snow was falling in the small unambitious way of late winter, and the road had climbed into country where the villages were few and far between, and the mountains had begun to appear — a pale smudge on the horizon at noon that became a definite line by dusk, and then a wall, and the wall had a name in the old tales, though no one on the road used it. Eldrin had been walking since before dawn, and his satchel had chosen that afternoon to make its displeasure known, the strap working a raw place through his coat at the shoulder, the weight settling into the hinge of his hip in a way that said *old man* in a voice he had been trying to ignore.

"Ho, the road!"

The voice came from behind him — or above him, or beside him; it arrived the way a cheerful voice arrives, from all the directions at once, out of the falling snow. Eldrin turned, and a man was there, closing the distance at a swinging pace, burdened with a pack that was visibly too large for him and visibly not slowing him down. He was younger than Eldrin by twenty years, brown-faced and wind-creased in the manner of men who live out of doors, and he was grinning with the particular idiocy of a man who has just walked his third hard day and has not yet noticed that he is tired.

"Hallo, hallo," the man said, arriving. "Forgive the hail — it's not every day I catch up with a fellow walker on this road, and I've been trying to catch up with you since the last village. You go at a proper pace. The pack's getting away from me." He laughed, and the laugh was a real thing, unhurried and whole. "I'm told there's a scholar walking east who knows the old roads. I've been hoping to meet him. The name's Oren. Oren of — well, of the road, lately. A chronicler, of a sort. I collect lost libraries."

The name landed, and the title landed, and Eldrin, who had spent three weeks being an unremarkable old man with a satchel, found that he did not have the heart to be unremarkable anymore. Or perhaps it was simpler than that. The man had a pack too heavy for him, and a laugh that was not performing, and a profession that was, of all professions, the one that would have made Eldrin himself on any other road of his life.

"A scholar," Eldrin said. "Of the old stones, mostly. The ruins draw me east."

"The ruins," Oren said, and his eyes — grey, quick, full of a genuine appetite — went past Eldrin to the pale line of the mountains. "There's a whole world in the ruins, if you know where to look. I've spent the winter in the libraries of the Silver Coast, chasing a collection that was supposed to have been moved east before the Gap. You know the kind of thing — a library that wasn't so much lost as *relocated*, and the relocation went unrecorded, and now the books are out there somewhere in a cellar or a shrine, waiting to be found." He said it with the plain unembarrassed hunger of a man who genuinely loves the thing he does. "But a collection is only as good as its road. You're heading east. So am I. The road is long, and the snow is doing what snow does. Walk with me a little, and I'll share my story, and you can share yours, and we'll both get there."

The offer was light, and it was made lightly, and Eldrin — who had spent three weeks walking alone with the needle in his skull and the record of the world in his satchel — found himself weighing it with an attention the offer did not deserve. A companion was a risk. He knew that; he had known it since the night the pack had taught him the price of being seen. A companion was a witness, and a witness was a question waiting to be asked, and the questions a witness asks are the ones that undo a careful life.

And yet. The man had grey quick eyes and a heavy pack and a profession of lost libraries, and he had offered to walk east, and the snow was falling, and the road was long, and Eldrin had been alone.

"The ruins," Eldrin said, "are a long way. You may not find your library there."

"I've walked further for worse," Oren said cheerfully. "And if I don't find it, I'll have found the road, and the road is the better part of a chronicle. What's a library, after all, but a road a man walks through with his eyes open?"

It was, Eldrin would think later, an extremely good answer. It was the answer of a man who meant it. And Eldrin, who had spent his whole life being right and alone, discovered in that moment that he was tired of being alone, and that being tired of being alone was not the same as being wrong.

"Walk, then," he said. "The road is wide enough for two. And you can tell me about your lost libraries."

Oren's grin widened. He fell into step — Eldrin noted, with the scholar's eye for detail, that the step matched his own, that the pack settled on the man's shoulders as if it had been carried a thousand miles, that the man walked with his weight forward and his eyes on the road ahead, the walk of a man who meant to arrive. "You'll have the whole of it," Oren said. "I'm a chronicler, which is to say I'm a man who's spent his life learning how much the world forgets, and I've decided to go and find the forgetting. The old kings — the ones who ruled before the Gap, the ones the old tales barely remember — their courts had libraries, Eldrin. Not little collections. *Libraries*. Rooms full of the world as it was actually run, before someone decided the record should begin again. And every one of them was tidied, the way you tidy a room when you're done with it. The books went somewhere. The tidy always leaves a trail, if you know how to read the trail."

Eldrin's step did not falter. He had not told the man his name, and the man had used it — but that was not, in itself, strange; the road had been full of people who had heard of the scholar of the old strata, and Oren had already said he had been trying to catch up with him. The man's enthusiasm was for the Gap, the same Gap that had eaten Eldrin's life. The coincidence was... pleasant. That was the word. It was pleasant to meet a man who spoke of the tidying as something that left a trail, instead of as a theory in a paper the college declined to print.

"You speak," Eldrin said, "of the erased record."

"Everyone speaks of it," Oren said. "That's the joke of it. The whole world knows the record was erased — they just call it history. But the *books*, Eldrin. Someone took the books. And if someone took them, someone knows where they are. And a chronicler who found them —" He shook his head, an expression of pure, uncomplicated longing on his face. "A chronicler who found them would be the richest man in the world. Not in money. In *story*. In the thing the world forgot it was."

They walked. The snow fell. The mountains grew. And Eldrin, who had come out of his tower expecting to walk the solitary road of the old tales — the road he had rehearsed in his study, the road of the man who walked alone because he had to — discovered that the road had other arrangements in mind.

It took him the rest of that day to admit it. Oren was relentless in the particular way of good company: he did not press, did not pry, did not perform. He simply *was* — a man walking east with a pack too heavy for him, full of the lore of ancient kings and vanished courts, able to spend half an hour on the subject of a minor chronicler's handwriting without once boring his listener, able to tell a funny story about a scribe and a weasel and a manuscript that would have made the college's most austere archivist laugh into his sleeve. He remembered the names of villages they passed and the names of the people in them. He split the firewood when they camped and made a stew that was, by the standards of the road, magnificent. And once, when a snow squall caught them in open country and Eldrin's raw shoulder gave out under the satchel's weight, Oren simply reached over and took the strap — not asking, not making a moment of it, carrying the load a mile and a half until the road shelved and the wind dropped, as if bearing another man's weight were the most natural errand in the world. He asked about Eldrin's studies with a genuine appetite, and he listened — truly listened, the way a chronicler listens, filing every detail away.

And he never once asked, in those first days, where Eldrin was going, or why, or what he hoped to find in the ruins. That was the thing, Eldrin realized later, that made him trust the man. Oren asked about everything else — the strata, the quarries, the theory of the Echoes, the old roads, the names of the stars — and he did not ask the one question that mattered. A spy would have asked. A spy would have wanted to know what the scholar of the old strata was doing walking east in winter. Oren, apparently, was content to walk.

By the fourth day, Eldrin had stopped guarding the fact that he was enjoying himself. He had not expected to. He had expected the road to be a penance, and instead it had produced, out of the falling snow, a companion who split wood properly and could quote a minor chronicler at length and laughed at his own bad jokes. It was not friendship, he told himself. It was the road's version of company — two men walking the same direction, trading the weight of their steps. The old tales called it friendship; the old tales were sentimental. He was a scholar; he knew better.

He did not know better. He knew, in fact, exactly the opposite, and the knowing was the thing that frightened him, because he had not been lonely before the road, and he had not known how much the loneliness was costing him until the cost had been lifted. Oren's company was not a luxury. It was a need, as physical as the need for food and warmth, and Eldrin — who had spent his life denying that he needed anything — was not prepared for it.

They reached the foot of the mountains on the ninth day. The snow had deepened; the road had narrowed; the villages had grown rare, and then rarer, and then stopped altogether, leaving only the road and the two of them and the pale wall of the mountains, rising into the low grey sky like the edge of the known world. Eldrin's shoulder had healed. His hip had made its peace with the weight. And in his skull, steady as a pulse, the needle pointed east, up into the pass, into the country where the maps ran out.

They camped that night in a shepherd's lean-to, long abandoned, its roof patched with birch-bark and its hearth ringed with the ash of a hundred cold fires. The wind had a sound in the pass that Eldrin had not heard before — a thin, continuous, almost musical note, the sound a blade makes when it is drawn and not yet used. They sat on opposite sides of the fire, and Oren was quiet for once, watching the flames with the particular stillness of a man who has been walking all day and has not yet been asked the question he has been expecting.

"Eldrin," Oren said, at last, in a tone that was softer than his usual voice. "I've been walking with you for nine days, and I've told you my whole story — my whole cover, as it were, the chronicler and the libraries and the silver coast — and you've told me about strata, and Echoes, and the quarries under the lake, and the college that wouldn't print your paper." He smiled. "All true things. I've no doubt they're all true. But I'm a chronicler, and a chronicler can tell when a man is walking with a story he isn't telling."

The fire moved. Eldrin's hand was very still on his staff.

"I'm not asking you to tell it," Oren said. "I wouldn't. A man's story is his own road; it's the one thing a chronicler doesn't borrow. I'm only asking — and I'll ask it once, and then I'll never ask it again — whether the road we're walking is going to be dangerous."

It was the right question. It was, Eldrin thought, the only right question a friend could ask. And because it was the right question, and because the man had split the firewood and made the stew and laughed at his own jokes for nine days, and because the fire was warm and the wind was thin and the loneliness had been lifted and he was grateful, Eldrin answered with more than he meant to.

"Yes," he said. "It is going to be dangerous. And I would not have you —" He stopped. He had been about to say *I would not have you killed for my sake*, and the sentence was truer than he wanted it to be, and the truth of it sat in the firelight between them like a third man.

Oren did not press the silence. He looked at Eldrin for a long moment, and the look was not the look of a man who wanted something; it was the look of a man who was taking the measure of a road he had decided to walk. "Then it's settled," he said, "that I walk it with you. You're a terrible liar, you know. You go pale when you tell a half-truth, and you've been going pale since the last village. I'm a chronicler; I notice these things." He grinned. "But I'll take the pale lies over the road alone, and I think you will too."

And Eldrin, against his better judgment — against every lesson the pack had taught him, against the voice in his head that said a man carrying the record of the world must not carry a witness too — felt the resistance go out of him the way a held breath goes out. He had been wrong to walk alone. He had been wrong the way a man is wrong when he mistakes the weight of the burden for the shape of the road. He had been walking east with a pack full of the world's only true record, and he had been doing it alone, and he had not known how the alone was costing him.

"Walk it with me, then," he said. "And we'll see what the mountains have to say about it."

Oren's grin, in the firelight, was the most honest thing Eldrin had seen since the blue sky. "The mountains," Oren said, "have been waiting for us."

He poked the fire, a small domestic gesture, and Eldrin's guard — already lowered, already gone slack with the warmth and the weariness and the relief of having spoken at last to someone — let him ask it. "I told you I wouldn't ask," Oren said, and the way he said it was idle, was exactly the way a chronicler says anything, "but I'm a chronicler, and a chronicler asks. What's at the end of the road, then, for you? You've been walking east in winter for a country you won't name. Even for a scholar of the old stones, that's a particular kind of faith. Is it a ruin? A door? A thing you mean to find, or a thing you mean to keep from being found?"

The question was natural. That was the trap of it, and Eldrin walked into it with the same step he had walked into the whole of the evening. A friend would ask it. A chronicler would ask it. It was the question the road had been asking him for weeks, only dressed in the voice of a man who had earned the right to ask it by splitting the firewood and bearing the load and staying cheerful through the snow. "A thing that was lost," he said. "I mean to see it found, or to see that it stays lost. I haven't decided which."

It was the truest half-truth he had told in his life, and Oren heard it, and Oren smiled — the same open, generous smile, the smile of a man who has been given a story and is savoring it — and he did not ask the next question. He let it go. He let it go so smoothly, so completely, that there was nothing for Eldrin's scholar's ear to catch, nothing to file, nothing to weigh.

And yet, as he banked the fire and the wind sang its thin note over the lean-to, Eldrin found himself thinking, in the small plain voice he used for heresy, that the question had been one question too many. Not because it was wrong. Because it was *right* — because a man who had been walking beside him for nine days without once asking where he was going should not have known, when he finally did ask, exactly where to press. The ease of it. The timing of it. The way it had waited until the fire was warm and the weariness was deep and the guard was down, and then arrived, perfectly aimed.

He dismissed it. He had to dismiss it, the way a man dismisses any thought that would make his solitude the only safe place in the world. The pack had taught him to be afraid of the dark; he was not going to let it teach him to be afraid of kindness too. The man had split the firewood. The man had carried his satchel through a snow squall. The man had laughed at his own bad jokes, and the laughter had been real, and Eldrin knew real laughter the way he knew the shape of his own doubt. He was a scholar. He dealt in evidence. And the evidence said that Oren was exactly what he appeared to be: a chronicler, a companion, a man walking east who had helped to bear the heavy load.

They slept. The wind sang its thin note. The fire went down. And in the small hours, when the fire was ash and the lean-to was full of the quiet breathing of a sleeping man who was not, as far as anyone could prove, a danger, Eldrin lay awake with his eyes closed and the needle in his skull.

It was trembling.

He had not felt it do that before. It had been steady as a pulse since the vision — fixed, patient, pointing east. And now, at the foot of the mountains, with the pass above them and the country of the maps' end ahead, the needle had begun to tremble, a fine continuous vibration, the way a needle trembles when the thing it points at grows near, or the way a wire trembles when a bell has been struck somewhere along its length.

Closer.

He did not know whether the trembling was distance closing or a warning being rung. He did not know whether the mountains had been waiting for them, or whether the mountains had been waiting for something else entirely. He knew only that the needle was trembling, and that the trembling meant the thing he had come to find was closer than he had believed, and that Oren, a few feet away, was breathing in the quiet rhythm of a man who was asleep or a man who was very, very good at pretending.

Eldrin opened his eyes. The lean-to was dark. The fire was ash. The mountains stood over them like a held breath, and somewhere beyond them, east, past the pass and the summit and the country where the maps ran out, the Heartstone of Creation lay in the keeping of the ruin — the thing that held, the anchor against the lifting of the floor — and the needle trembled, and the mountains were close, and he was not alone.

He closed his eyes, and the mountains stood over them like a held breath, and he slept.
