# Ch. 4 — Odyssey's Dawn
<!-- Track: 3. Odyssey's Dawn | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, winter -->

He left Thaloria by the East Gate at dawn, when the bell tower was sounding its hour with the serene inaccuracy that had been its habit all his life, and the door of the tower was sealed behind him — he had turned the key himself, before the snow had stopped, and the sound of the lock had been the sound of a ledger closing. The city lay quiet in its celebrated peace, and he walked out of it, and no one stopped him, and no one asked where he was going, because no one in Thaloria had ever asked a scholar where he was going. That was the city's one mercy.

The sky was a blue he had never seen before.

He had seen the sky every day of sixty years. He had written papers on the behavior of light in the high quarries; he had a theory about the precise angle at which the winter sun lost its authority; he had watched ten thousand dawns break over the towers of the scholar-city without once noticing that they were beautiful, because beauty was a property of things you could annotate. This sky was not annotable. It was blue — not the tired blue of early winter, but a blue washed clean, a blue poured out fresh, a blue that had no idea what a violet sky was, or a bruise, or a wound, or a world that had ended before and would end again. It was the blue of a world that had not yet heard the news.

Terrifying. That was the only word for it, and he did not know why it was the only word for it until he had walked half a mile and let the thought settle. The blue was terrifying because it did not know. It had never been told. It was the sky of a world that believed the century would go on being silent, and the peace would go on being peace, and the seams would go on being hidden. He was carrying the only copy of the true record, and the record said the blue was a lie, and the blue did not know it was a lie, and the blue was beautiful anyway.

He walked east.

The road was a winter road, hard-packed and frosted, the wheel-ruts of the autumn harvest frozen into pale lines that ran ahead of him like a promise. The needle lay in his skull, steady as a pulse, ticking east. He did not need to consult it; he could no more have failed to know where the Heartstone was than he could have failed to know where his own feet were. That was the strangeness of the first day, and it never quite wore off: he was a man who had spent his life finding his way by maps and marginalia, and now he was being carried by something he could not see, the way a boat is carried by a current, and the current did not require his opinion.

The first village came at noon.

It was exactly what the old tales called a village of wood and thatch — a dozen houses thatched against the winter, a smithy, a shrine with its roof freshly mended, a bell that had not yet learned Thaloria's bad habits. Smoke stood straight up in the cold. A woman was drawing water at the well; an old man was mending harness in the doorway of the smithy; children were running the length of the single street with the particular shameless velocity of children who have no idea that they are being watched by the future. None of them looked up at the sky. None of them had any reason to. The fire in the sky was a thing that had happened once, long ago, and been tidied away, and the tidying had been so thorough that even the memory of the question had been cleaned off the shelves.

He passed through the village the way a scholar passes through any place he does not intend to annotate, at a steady walking pace, looking at the rooftops and the well and the children with the appraising courtesy of a man who has learned to see without staring. And the village, without a word being said, moved around him.

It was not rudeness. It was not fear, not exactly. It was a *misgiving*, a soft animal adjustment, the way a herd of cattle will part around a man who has been in the company of wolves without being able to say why. The children slowed as he passed, and their games lost their momentum, and one small girl stood in the middle of the street with her hands at her sides, watching him with an expression that was not curiosity but assessment, as if she were trying to place a smell. The woman at the well did not smile. The old man in the smithy door let his hands go still on the harness and did not resume until Eldrin had gone past the forge and well out of reach of the threshold.

He did not stop. He did not turn around. He filed it, the way he filed everything, and he kept walking, and in the evening he wrote it down in the second journal, in the small neat hand he reserved for evidence:

*First village. They do not know the sky. They do not know the fire that is coming. And they are uneasy around me — all of them, without exception, without a cause they could name. I have begun to think I carry something into a room before I enter it, the way a man carries a draft under his coat. I do not know what it is. I do not know if it was in me before the vision or put there by it. I suspect — though I will set this down as suspicion and not finding — that it is the same quality that made the storm choose me: I am the place the Aether touches most closely in this thin age, and things that live on attention can feel me approaching.*

*I will have to learn to walk quietly in the world.*

He had written it, and the writing had made it more true, which was the way writing worked, and he had gone to sleep that night in a hollow under a shelf of rock with no fire, because the first night out he had not yet learned how to be cold, and the cold had taught him.

The next day passed through a second village and a third, and the pattern held, and he refined his understanding of it. It was not that the villagers disliked him. They did not know him. It was that they could not quite keep their attention on the ordinary business of their lives while he was among them, and they did not know why, and they had no vocabulary for the unease, and so they were, if anything, more polite than they would have been to a stranger they could file. They offered him bread and questioned him closely about the roads, and when he had gone they went back to their lives with the faint relieved air of men who have shut a door. He was a lighthouse, he decided. That was the word for it. A lighthouse does not mean to be frightening, and it does not shine to warn anyone, and the ships that pass it at night steer a little wide without knowing why.

On the third day, the road took him through the bare margin of a wood — not Whisper-Wood, thank the small mercies, but a copse of winter birches that ran beside the road for a mile and then gave way to open country — and the road, which had been a trade road, began to empty. The carts had turned south toward the market. The travelers had found their destinations. And Eldrin, who had been walking east with the needle steady in his skull, found himself at dusk with the light going out of the day and the question of where to sleep still unsettled.

He should have stopped earlier. He knew it, the way a man knows he should have mended the roof in autumn. He had been walking too fast, pushed by a current that did not require his opinion, and he had let the light get ahead of him, and the copse of birches had thickened without being consulted, and the road had narrowed, and the dusk had come down the way dusk comes in winter — not gradually, but as a decision.

He built a small fire. That was his first mistake, and he made it carefully, in a clearing a few strides off the road, with dry wood and a ring of stones, and he sat beside it with his back to a tree and ate the bread the second village had given him and watched the dark come up out of the ground. The fire was small. The dark was large. He had spent sixty winters knowing the dark was large and the fire was small and that this was the natural arrangement of things, and he had never once before felt the dark as *attentive*.

He felt it now. It had a grain to it, the way the silence of the city had developed a grain in the months before the letter. He had learned to read that grain — a texture in the air like glass about to give — and he read it now, sitting by the fire in the copse of birches, and what he read was wrong.

He did not know what the sound was, at first. It was not a howl. He had heard wolves; the high quarries had taught him wolves, the long argument of a pack staking its claim to the night. This was not that. This was a *patter*, a soft many-footed percussion, moving through the birches with a cadence that did not match the ground — now left, now right, now too close, now farther, in a way that made no geometry. It was the sound of a pack that had learned the shape of wolves and not the soul of them.

He rose. The staff was in his hand — it had a way of being in his hand when he needed it, a habit thirty years of stairs had given it — and he stood with his back to the fire and turned the circle of his attention outward, and the birches gave back nothing but the dark.

Then the dark moved.

It came out of the treeline in a line — four of them, then seven, then more, shambling forward with a gait that was almost wolflike and was wrong in the way a copied signature is wrong, fluent and soulless at once. They were the color of the shadows between birches, which is to say they were the color of nothing that can be named, and their eyes caught the firelight at angles that should not have been possible, glinting green-and-violet. They did not growl. Wolves growl; these things had nothing in them that needed to growl. They simply *arrived*, pouring out of the dark the way a stain pours through cloth, and they stood at the edge of the firelight, and they looked at him, and their attention was the same attention he had felt all his life from the Aether — the attention of something that wanted the warmth.

He had read about them. The bestiary of the old world, the one that had survived in the Archive's less-tidied corners, had a page on them, and the page had been annotated by a hunter who had not been a scholar and had therefore told the truth about what he had seen. *Shadow-touched,* the hunter had written, in a hand that shook. *Wolves that were. The leech-corruption gets into a beast and it keeps the shape and loses the reason. They move at odd angles. They come to warmth. They will not die to steel the way a wolf dies, and they do not like the light.*

They did not like the light. That was the page he had, and it was the only page he had, and the pack stood at the edge of his small fire and did not like the light, and the fire was small, and the dark was large, and he was sixty years old and had never once in his life killed anything larger than a moth.

He could have run. He did not consider it; the consideration was not offered. The pack had him ringed, and the ring was the work of a thing that knew its business, and the fire was the only thing between him and the dark, and the fire was dying.

The rune-kit was in his satchel. He had carried it for thirty years and used it for the small geometries of scholarship — the sealing of a crate, the warding of a drawer, the polite discouragement of mold — and he had never once drawn a ward in anger, and he had never once drawn one in the presence of something that wanted him dead.

He did not think about it. He dropped to one knee — the ground was frozen, and the chalk was in his hand, and his hand did not shake, which surprised him more than anything that followed — and he drew.

A circle. That was the first figure, and the oldest, the boundary that says *here the world changes hands*. A square within it, rotated a hair off true, because a ward that is perfectly true is a ward that can be read, and a ward that can be read can be unmade. The interlocking rings he had incised along the staff as a youth, the figure the masters called the *lock* and the students called the *knot*: the geometric statement that this air, in this circle, belonged to him. He drew it in chalk, and the chalk was cold, and his breath smoked, and the pack came forward a step, and another, and the lead creature's eyes caught the firelight in their wrong green-violet way, and he finished the figure and fed it.

The feeding was a wrenching. He had no better word for it, then or after. His soul-energy went out of him the way water goes out of a cracked jug — not poured, *drawn*, by a figure that knew what it was for — and the chalk lines took the color of the energy, and the energy was blue, and the blue was violet at the edges, the exact violet of the ink he used for heresy, and the lines *lit*.

The light was not firelight. It was a cleaner thing, a geometric thing, a thing that had corners, and the pack hit it as a wave hits a seawall, and the wall did not yield. The lead creature — the largest, the one whose eyes burned greenest — came at the circle where the chalk line crossed the frozen earth, and the line met it, and the line did not break, and the creature did not stop, and the thing that happened to it there at the boundary was the thing that happens to a moth on a candle-flame, only slower, only more specific. It screamed. That was the first sound Eldrin had heard from them — a thin, tearing, wet scream, the sound of something that had forgotten it could feel and had just been reminded — and it reeled back, and the light of the ward burned in its eye-sockets for a moment and then went out, and it fell, and it did not rise.

The pack did not flee. That was the horror of it, the detail he would carry. Wolves flee from a burning brand. These things looked at their fallen, and their green-violet eyes moved to him, and they came forward again, around the circle, spreading out to ring it, testing it at a dozen points at once, and the ward held, and it held, and it held — and every time it held, it *burned*, and every burn was a beacon, and the beacon was not calling the pack, which was already there.

It was calling the rest of the dark.

He felt it in the Aether before he saw it: the ripples going out from the lit circle the way rings go out from a stone dropped in still water, and the dark *answering* them, the way still water answers, the way the deep answers a bell. From the trees behind the pack came more shapes. Not wolves — something taller, something that had learned the shape of wolves from a greater distance, something that walked on two legs in the memory of four. And in the Aether, at the edge of his own burning, he felt the vast unhurried attention of the night turning toward him, the way a great slow eye turns toward a struck match in a long dark room.

The lesson was not the ward. The lesson was the light.

He broke the circle. Not carefully — deliberately, with the toe of his boot, scuffing the chalk line across where it met the earth, and the violet guttered and went out, and the dark came back in like a tide, and he was standing in the sudden night with a pack of shadow-touched things between him and every direction and no light left to hold them.

He did not run. He *walked* — east, the needle's east, into the dark, away from the dying smell of his own burnt ward, away from the small ring of stones that had held his fire. The pack did not pursue. That was the mercy he had not expected, and he did not understand it until much later, when he understood what the light had been: it had been the *advertisement*. The things that came to a burning ward had not come to kill him. They had come to drink the light, to stand in the warmth of a soul that had been opened and left open. And when the light went out, the party was over, and the guests had gone home to the deep, disappointed, hungry, patient.

He walked until his legs would not carry him, and then he walked a little more, and when he could not walk he sat down in the road with his back to a frost-split stone and his staff across his knees, and he was shaking. Not with fear — with the cost. The ward had taken from him more than chalk and concentration. The world had gone grey around him, and his hands had a distance to them, and his heart was beating as if it were trying to remember a rhythm it had almost forgotten. He had read about this. The old texts called it the Snap, the stage of exhaustion when the soul's anchor to the body grows thin, and the body begins to wonder whether it is still wanted.

He had been a mage all his life and never once spent his soul-energy in anger. He had been taught that magic was a debt. He had been taught that it drew things. He had believed it, the way you believe a thing you have never had occasion to test, the way you believe a stove is hot without putting your hand on it. And now he had put his hand on it, and the stove had been hot, and the hand was not going to be the same, and neither was he.

*Magic is expensive,* he wrote, later, in the second journal, in the grey first light of the fourth day, with his hand shaking from cold and cost. *It costs what it costs, and then it costs more, because it calls to everything that feeds on cost. I will use it as a man uses money on a long road: sparingly, in the dark, and never twice in the same place.*

He closed the journal. He sat in the grey dawn with the needle steady in his skull, ticking east, and he took account of himself the way a merchant takes account at the end of a bad season. He was not a mage of consequence. He was not a soldier. He was a man of sixty years with a satchel, a staff, and a secret so large that it had rearranged the sky, and the world had just taught him, in the only language it respected, that he could not afford to be seen.

He would travel as a wanderer-scholar. No titles. No city, no college, no name that anyone would repeat. A scholar studying the old stones and the older roads, walking east, patient, unremarkable, a man with a satchel and a staff and a small tidy manner — the kind of man the world files and forgets.

The compass pulled east. He got up, and he went.
