# Ch. 22 — The Return
<!-- Track: 11. The Weight of Eternity | POV: Eldrin | Date: 501 AGD, autumn → winter -->

He carried the stone wrapped in the last of his oilcloth, and the oilcloth lay against his chest under his coat, and the pulse of it beat against his ribs the way a second heart beats against a man's own, and he walked west out of the valley of the ruins and the world closed around him like a country that had not noticed he was gone.

That was the whole strangeness of the return, and it was the first lesson of the vigil, and it would be the last lesson of it: the world had gone on. He had walked out of it in the early winter of the year, and the world had not paused to watch him go, and now he walked back into it in the autumn of the same year, carrying the thing that held the world shut at its one seam, and the world did not pause to watch him come. The fire's country recovered. The villages of the lowlands went about their living. The road ran west under a sky that had never once been the color of a held breath, and the sky did not know the name of the thing he carried, and the sky did not need to know.

The pulse went with him. That was the second lesson, and it was the stranger one: the pulse of the stone had become the pulse of him, and the two of them beat together the way they had beat together in the chamber, and the beating had a geography now. He could feel the seam — the Lock, the man at the end of the world's long holding — the way a man feels a weight he has taken up without meaning to, not as a burden, as a *bearing*, the steady knowledge of the thing that is being held. The stone held the seam from wherever he held the stone, and he held the stone, and the holding was the whole of the work, and the work had begun.

He crossed the fire's country in the slow red autumn, and the ash of the Pyre-Lord's ending had gone to grass. The ridge of black glass ran away south of him, and the mountain stood grey beyond it, and the country between the burning and the ruin had healed the way the world healed — not back to what it had been, forward, into what it would be — and he walked through the middle of the healing and did not stop, because the road did not stop and the vigil did not stop and the pulse did not stop.

He came up into the mountains before the first snow, and the mountains were the mountains of the winter road, the country of the ascent and the summit, and they were not the same mountains he had climbed. He had climbed them in the deep of the winter with a traveler at his side and a mask on the traveler's face, and he had been betrayed at the gate and burned in the labyrinth and changed at the mountain's heart, and the mountains had been the trial and the trial had been the road and the road was over now. He walked them as a man walks a country he has already crossed — not with the sharp attention of the traveler, with the settled weight of the one who has been through them — and the mountains let him pass the way they let pass the ones who are no longer being tested.

The green valley came before the last of the passes, and the valley had changed.

That was the third strangeness of the return, and he stood at the edge of it for a long time before he went down into it. The green valley was not green. The impossible early-spring of the year before, the valley that had bloomed green amidst the snow, had gone the way of the season — the leaves had turned, the light had thinned, the Elemental's kept warmth had drawn in for the winter the way the forest drew in, and the green had become the country of the autumn, deep and patient and old. He walked down into it the way a man walks into a room he has left, and the forest recognized him — he felt it in the turn of the light, in the settling of the leaves, in the way the path opened ahead of him as if it remembered his feet — and the hut stood at the heart of the green where it had stood, grown of living wood and living leaf, with the fire burning in its ring of stones and the fire did not smoke.

The Hermit sat by the fire, and the Hermit was not surprised.

"I felt you come," the hermit said. His voice was the same, the grain of a thing that had been speaking to itself for a long time, and his eyes were the color of the river in the shallows, and they looked at the oilcloth at Eldrin's chest the way a keeper looks at the thing he once kept. "Felt you through the green, the way a pond feels a stone. You have it, then. You stood before the door, and the door opened, and the thing that kept it let you carry it out." He did not ask how. He was the one who had been defeated by the thing at the door, and he knew the only way past it, and he had told it to the one man who had been able to use it.

"It accepted me," Eldrin said. He did not know why he said it, or why the saying of it came out the way a confession comes out — not as a boast, as a record, as the thing he needed the one other keeper in the world to hear. "The stone read my hand the way you said it would. The Balrog —" He stopped. The word was too small, and the encounter was too large, and the Hermit was the one man alive who would understand the size of both.

"The Balrog is the kind of thing that keeps," the hermit said. "It kept the door for the keeping, and the keeping has a keeper now, and the keeper is the one thing its keeping could not refuse." He looked at Eldrin, and the river-colored eyes were very old, and there was no envy in them and no regret, and the not-regretting was the whole of the forgiveness the green valley had to give. "You have the vigil now, scholar. The one I could not hold. The one I was broken by." He paused, and the fire moved, and the river turned its page. "I have been a forest for longer than I was a keeper, and I have learned the one thing the keeping never taught me: the vigil is not a punishment, and it is not a glory, and it is not anything the world will ever see. It is the weight of a held breath. And you are holding it."

"I am holding it," Eldrin said.

The hermit nodded, once, a slow bark-colored nod. "Then go home. Carry it where the world cannot reach it and where you will not be parted from it, and hold it through the years, and when the years are done and the hero comes for the key, you will know the holding was not for nothing." He looked away, at the fire, at the river, at the green going down into winter. "I will not see it. The forest will outlive me the way it outlived the keeper, and the valley will keep the record the way it kept mine. But the record will have a true page in it now, and that is more than I had when I failed."

Eldrin stayed one night in the green valley, and he did not sleep, and the Hermit did not sleep, and the two keepers sat by the fire that did not smoke through the whole of the night, and the pulse of the stone beat between them like the note of a thing that had finally been taken up by the right hand. In the grey of the morning he left, and the forest let him go, and the green closed behind him the way a door closes on a room he had been glad of.

He came down out of the mountains into the western lowlands in the first true snow of the winter, and the world of the year five hundred opened around him like the ordinary country it was.

The villages of wood and thatch. The trade roads. The inns where the travelers sat and the fires burned and the world went on the way it had always gone on, not knowing that a man carried the world's ending in an oilcloth at his chest. He passed through them the way he had passed through them a year before, but the passing was not the same, and the not-sameness was the fourth strangeness of the return, and it was the one that would not leave him.

He was blind to it now.

Not blind the way the name the world would give him would mean — not blind in the eyes, not blind in the flesh. He could see the villages as clearly as he had ever seen them, more clearly, with the exactness of a man who had learned to read the writing beneath the writing. He saw the children in the doorways and the mothers at the wells and the old men at the fires, and he saw the whole of them the way the timeline saw the whole of them: as lives that would be lived out and ended in the ordinary way, generation after generation, before the year nine hundred, before the hero, before the key. The girl who drew water at the well would grow and marry and die and be forgotten. The boy who ran shouting after the geese would be an old man when the century turned, and the old man's grandchildren would be old men when the world's ending came due. He saw them all, and the seeing was not a curse and not a gift — it was the perspective of the vigil, the view from the year nine hundred looking back, and he was standing in the middle of it, the only man alive who could see the whole of the distance at once.

They could not see him back. That was the whole of it, and he understood it the way he had understood the ward and the keeping and the weight of eternity: the world of the year five hundred could not see him, because the man who carried the thing that held the world was not a thing the world was looking for. He was an old man in a grey coat walking west in the first snow, with nothing to mark him, with no message and no question and no name, and the villages let him pass the way they let pass the years — without knowing what was passing.

He reached the road where the traveler had joined him in the last week of the return, and he stopped in the middle of it, and he stood for a long time in the falling snow.

It was an ordinary stretch of road — the same road he had walked a year before, the same ditches and hedges and the same grey sky, the place where the traveler had come up over the rise with his chronicle and his warmth and his mask made of true things. He had not meant to stop there. The road had stopped him, the way a wound stops a man, and he stood in the middle of it and weighed the year that had passed since the traveler had joined him.

He could not tell anyone. That was the fifth strangeness, and it was the one he would carry the longest, and he stood in the falling snow and felt the shape of it like a stone: he could not tell anyone. Not the villages he passed, not the keepers of the green valley, not the scholars of Thaloria, not the brother at Silverrun who had written the letter. He could not tell them what he carried, and he could not tell them what he was becoming, and he could not tell them that the traveler who had walked this road with him had worn a mask made of true things and had struck with the dagger that the shadows bring. He could not tell them the weight, because the weight was the vigil, and the vigil was his, and the telling would not have lifted it, it would have only made the weight theirs.

He stood in the middle of the road where the traveler had joined him, and the snow fell, and the world went on being the ordinary world, and he was the only man alive who knew the whole of the distance between the year that had been and the year that was coming, and he could not tell anyone.

He walked on. He had not looked back since the tower, and he did not look back now, and the road ran west through the villages and the trade roads and the inns, and the pulse of the stone beat against his ribs, and the world of the year five hundred went on living its oblivious life around him, and he moved through the middle of it like a man moving through the middle of a held breath — present, invisible, the one thing the world was not looking for.

Thaloria rose out of the snow on the last day of the return.

He had not known he was looking for it until he saw it — the scholar-city of his whole life, the towers and the archives and the bell tower whose hour had not struck correctly in his lifetime, standing on its hill above the river the way it had stood when he had left it, the way it had stood when he had been born under it, the way it would stand for the generations he was about to outlast. The city had not changed. That was the last strangeness of the return, and it was the one he had not been prepared for, because he had spent four hundred years of the future measuring the world and had not once stopped to measure the fact that the world of his own year would be standing exactly as he had left it.

He came up through the streets of the city in the winter dusk, and the streets were the streets of his life — the college gates, the archives, the houses of the scholars with their lamps coming on against the dark — and the city did not know him. He had been gone a year, and the city had not noticed, and the not-noticing was not a cruelty, it was the ordinary mercy of a world that could not see what it was not looking for. He passed a scholar he had known for forty years, and the scholar looked through him the way the ordinary world looked through the man who carried the thing that held it, and Eldrin did not stop, because the stopping would have required the telling, and the telling was the one thing the vigil would not allow.

The tower stood at the edge of the scholar's quarter as he had left it.

He stopped at the foot of it, in the snow, with the oilcloth at his chest and the pulse beating against his ribs, and he looked up at the tower that had been his whole life — the narrow windows, the stone stairs, the door he had sealed a year before with the runes of the scholars, knowing he might never return. The runes were dead. That was the first thing he noted, the scholar's note: the door stood as he had sealed it, and the sealing had held, and nothing had come or gone through it in a year, and the tower had stood through the winter and the spring and the summer and the autumn of the world without him, the way it had stood through the whole of his life, the way it would stand through the years he was about to keep.

He climbed the stairs. The door opened at his touch the way it had opened when he had left it — the runes coming up blue and patient under his palm, the old magic of his trade answering the hand that had made it — and he stepped through into the cold dark of the tower, and he closed the door behind him, and the tower closed around him the way the keeping had closed around the stone, and he stood in the middle of his own cold room with the strata cores in their slots and the journals on their shelves and the dust of a year on everything, and the pulse of the stone beat against his ribs like the note of a thing that had finally come home.

He had returned.

The vigil had begun.
