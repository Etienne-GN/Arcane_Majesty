# Chapter 3: The Blood Ritual

The first feeding was an abomination.

They had tried to resist. Acheron had instructed them to wait, to focus, to hold the Thirst at bay through discipline and will. But the Thirst was not a thing that could be held at bay through will alone. It was a force of nature — a hunger that gnawed at the edges of the mind, that whispered in the blood, that grew stronger with every passing moment.

By the third day, they could resist no longer.

Acheron led them to the forest at night, when the mortal world was asleep and the shadows were long. The moon was full, its light filtering through the canopy of ancient trees, casting silver patterns on the forest floor. The air was cool and sweet with the scent of growing things, and the silence was absolute, broken only by the distant sound of running water.

"Find prey," Acheron said. "But do not kill. Take only what you need, and no more."

They scattered into the darkness.

Acheron watched them go, his silver eyes tracking their movements through the shadows. They were fast — faster than any mortal, faster than any creature of the forest. The void energy had enhanced their bodies, sharpened their senses, honed their instincts. They were predators now, creatures of the night, and the forest was their hunting ground.

The sounds came quickly.

A scream, cut short. A gasp, silenced. The wet, tearing sound of flesh being pierced, of blood being drawn, of life being siphoned from the living. Acheron closed his eyes and felt the energy of the Void pulse through him — the same energy that flowed through the veins of his creations, the same hunger that drove them to feed.

The Thirst was awakening in him too.

He had not been transformed, not in the way the four had been. He was still a Seer, still a creature of the celestial realm, still carrying the light of Auroria in his blood. But the Tome had changed him, in its own way. The equations hummed in his mind, the patterns vibrated in his bones, the blueprints pulsed in his blood. He was connected to the Void now, connected to Nyktoros, connected to the dormant intelligence that slumbered beneath the fabric of existence.

And he was hungry.

The others returned, one by one, their silver eyes burning with the afterglow of feeding. Their skin was flushed, their movements fluid, their hunger temporarily sated. They stood before Acheron in the moonlight, their faces painted with shadows, their expressions a mixture of satisfaction and shame.

"It is done," Lyra said. Her voice was steady, but her hands trembled.

"How do you feel?" Acheron asked.

"Alive," Kael said. "More alive than I have ever felt."

"Hungry," Darius said. He grinned, his teeth sharp in the moonlight. "But satisfied."

"Disgusted," Elara said. Her voice was flat, clinical. "I fed on a human. I took their life force. I am a parasite."

"You are a survivor," Acheron said.

"I am a monster."

"You are what I made you."

Elara stared at him, her eyes hard. "Then you are a monster too."

The words hung in the air like a judgment. Acheron did not respond. He could not. She was right. He had created them, knowing the cost, knowing the Thirst, knowing the hunger that would drive them to feed on others. He had done it because he believed in the transformation, in the transcendence, in the chance to give mortals the power to endure.

But endurance came at a price.

And the price was blood.

---

The weeks that followed were a education.

Acheron taught them to control the Thirst — to feed without killing, to take only what they needed, to resist the hunger that gnawed at the edges of their minds. It was not easy. The Thirst was a force of nature, a hunger that grew stronger with every feeding, a craving that whispered in the blood and hummed in the bones.

But they learned.

Lyra was the quickest. She had a scholar's mind, trained to observe, to analyze, to understand. She studied the Thirst the way she had studied the old texts in the village archive — with detachment, with curiosity, with the determination to master what she did not understand. She learned to control the feeding, to take only what she needed, to resist the hunger that drove the others to excess.

Kael was the steadiest. He had a warrior's discipline, forged in the border skirmishes, tempered by the blood and the mud and the screams of the dying. He did not fight the Thirst — he accepted it, acknowledged it, and then put it aside, the way he put aside the fear and the pain and the exhaustion of battle. He fed when he needed to, and he did not enjoy it.

Darius was the most dangerous. He was young, angry, hungry for power. The Thirst fed his anger, amplified his hunger, drove him to excess. He fed often, and deeply, taking more than he needed, savoring the sensation of life flowing into him. Acheron warned him, counseled him, threatened him. Darius smiled and did not change.

Elara was the most conflicted. She was a scholar, trained to question, to doubt, to seek the truth even when the truth was uncomfortable. She fed because she had to, because the Thirst demanded it, because her body would not survive without the life force of others. But she hated herself for it. She hated the hunger, hated the dependency, hated the creature she had become.

She hated Acheron.

He could see it in her eyes — the cold, clinical hatred of a scholar who has been lied to, who has been manipulated, who has been used. She had accepted the transformation because she believed in the transcendence, in the chance to become something more than mortal. But the transcendence was a lie. The transformation was a trap. The Thirst was the price, and the price was too high.

Acheron could not blame her.

He hated himself too.

---

The first killing came in the fourth week.

It was Darius.

He had been feeding too often, too deeply, taking more than he needed. The Thirst had grown in him, fed by his anger, amplified by his hunger. He had become reckless, careless, sloppy. And on a night when the moon was dark and the shadows were deep, he drained a human completely.

The body was found at dawn, pale and empty, its life force siphoned away. The village was thrown into panic. The elders spoke of demons, of dark creatures, of the things that lurked in the forest at night. They armed themselves with torches and swords and went hunting.

Acheron was furious.

"You were told not to kill," he said, his voice cold with rage. "You were told to feed only what you needed. You were told to control the Thirst."

Darius shrugged, his silver eyes gleaming in the darkness. "I was hungry."

"You were reckless. You were careless. You have drawn attention to us."

"Let them come." Darius grinned, his teeth sharp in the moonlight. "We are stronger than they are. We are faster. We are *better*. What do we have to fear from mortals?"

"Everything," Acheron said. "They are many. We are few. If they discover what we are, they will hunt us. They will destroy us."

"They can try."

"They will succeed."

Darius stared at him, his eyes hard. "You are afraid."

"Yes. I am afraid. And you should be too."

"Of mortals?" Darius laughed. "We are beyond mortals. We are beyond their reach. We are *gods*."

The word hung in the air like a curse.

Acheron looked at Darius — at his young face, his sharp teeth, his silver eyes burning with the afterglow of feeding. He saw what he had created: a creature of hunger, of instinct, of the endless, empty Thirst that could never be satisfied. He had given Darius the power to endure, and Darius had used it to kill.

"This is not what I intended," Acheron said.

"It is exactly what you intended," Darius said. "You created us to be predators. You gave us the Thirst. You taught us to feed. And now you are surprised when we act like predators?"

"I taught you to control the Thirst."

"Control is for the weak. We are strong."

Acheron closed his eyes. The darkness behind his eyelids was absolute, and the equations of the Tome hummed in his blood, the patterns vibrating at frequencies that mortal ears could not parse. He had created Darius, knowing the cost, knowing the Thirst, knowing the hunger that would drive him to excess. He had done it because he believed in the transformation, in the transcendence, in the chance to give mortals the power to endure.

But endurance came at a price.

And the price was blood.

"What will you do?" Darius asked.

Acheron opened his eyes. The forest was dark, the moon was hidden, and the silence was absolute.

"I will do what I must," he said.

He raised his hand, and the void energy surged through him — the same energy that flowed through the veins of his creations, the same hunger that drove them to feed. It was not a power he had been born with. It was a power he had stolen, copied from the blueprints of Nyktoros, siphoned from the Phantom Realm. It was forbidden, dangerous, monstrous.

But it was *his*.

The energy struck Darius like a wave, slamming him backward, pinning him against the trunk of an ancient tree. He screamed — a raw, animal sound that echoed off the canopy. Acheron's silver eyes burned with the light of the Void, and his voice was cold as the void between worlds.

"You will never kill again," he said. "You will never feed beyond what you need. You will never draw attention to us, or to the Covenant that I am building. You will control the Thirst, or you will be destroyed."

Darius struggled against the energy, his silver eyes wide with fear. For the first time since his transformation, he looked like what he was: a creature of hunger, of instinct, of the endless, empty Thirst that could never be satisfied.

"Please," he whispered. "Please, I will control it. I will be better. I will—"

"You will die," Acheron said.

The energy surged, and Darius screamed, and the forest fell silent.

When it was over, Acheron stood alone in the darkness, the body at his feet, the equations of the Tome humming in his blood. He had killed his creation. He had destroyed the first vampire that he had made.

The cost of control was blood.

And the blood was his to shed.

---

The others found him at dawn.

Lyra was first. She saw the body, saw Acheron's face, saw the equations still humming in the air. She did not speak. She simply stood beside him, her silver eyes fixed on the horizon, and waited.

Kael came next. He looked at the body, looked at Acheron, and nodded. He understood. The warrior's code demanded it — the death of the unruly, the destruction of the disobedient. It was harsh. It was necessary.

Elara came last. She looked at the body, looked at Acheron, and her face was a mask of cold fury.

"You killed him," she said.

"He was a danger to us."

"You killed your own creation."

"He was a danger to *all* of us."

Elara stared at him, her eyes hard. "You are a monster."

"Yes," Acheron said. "I am."

The words hung in the air like a confession. The forest was silent, the sky was blue, and the world was the same as it had been the day before. But something had changed. Something fundamental, something irreversible.

Acheron had killed.

He had created life, and he had destroyed it. He had given the mortals the power to endure, and he had taken it away. He had opened a door that could not be closed, and now he stood at the threshold, watching the darkness gather.

The Covenant was born in blood.

And the blood would never dry.
