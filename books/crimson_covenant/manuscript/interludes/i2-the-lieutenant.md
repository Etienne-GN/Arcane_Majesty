# Interlude II: The Lieutenant

Vorenus stood in the ruins of the Sanguine Vaults and watched the energy die.

It happened in silence — not the clean, decisive silence of a blade falling or a door closing, but the slow, creeping silence of a heartbeat fading. The conduits that lined the walls had once pulsed with a light that was not light, a luminescence born of the Phantom Realm, bleeding through the cracks in reality and into the stone. For centuries, that light had filled the tunnels, had painted the chambers in hues of silver and violet, had hummed at frequencies that mortal ears could not parse but that vampires felt in their blood and their bones and the hollow spaces behind their eyes. It had been the voice of the Vaults, the pulse of the Covenant, the breath of an age that had promised eternity.

Now it was dying.

Vorenus watched the light recede. It pulled back from the walls like a tide retreating from the shore, leaving behind stone that was cold and dead and ordinary. The channels carved into the rock — channels that Acheron had designed with the precision of a scholar directing the flow of an equation — went dark, one by one, like stars winking out at the end of the world. The luminescence did not simply vanish; it withered, dimming in stages, the silver fading to grey, the violet bleeding to black, until the only light left was the faint, phosphorescent glow of lichen on ancient stone. The hum that had thrummed in the walls for centuries, a low and constant vibration that had been as much a part of the Vaults as the stone itself, faltered. Stuttered. Grew quieter. And then, with a final, almost imperceptible sigh — like the last breath of a dying god — it stopped.

Silence.

Not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of absence — the silence that follows the loss of something that should have been permanent, something that was supposed to endure. The conduits were sealed. The vaults were empty. The pulse that had hummed in the walls for centuries was silent. The Soul-Leak was dead, and with it, the sustenance that had kept the Thirst at bay.

The hunger returned.

Vorenus felt it immediately — not gradually, not as a slow awareness creeping in from the margins of his consciousness, but as a physical thing, a pressure in his chest, a heat in his blood, a need that was as fundamental as breathing and far more terrible. It was the Thirst, the Void's hook embedded in the flesh of every vampire who had ever accepted the Primal Pact. For centuries, the Soul-Leak had kept it muted, a whisper behind a wall of borrowed energy. Now the wall was gone, and the whisper was a scream.

He stood in the darkness and felt the world change around him. The Vaults, which had been a sanctuary, a city, a home, became something else in the space of a heartbeat. They became a tomb — a vast, empty tomb, its walls lined with the dead channels of the Soul-Leak, its chambers filled with dust and silence. The energy that had once filled them, that had made them beautiful and alive and purposeful, was gone. The light was gone. The hum was gone. The sustenance that had kept the Thirst at bay was gone.

Everything was gone.

And the hunger was there.

Vorenus closed his eyes and let the hunger wash over him. It was not a new feeling — he had known it since the first night of his transformation, since the fire of the Blood Ritual had torn through his flesh and rewritten his nature. The hunger was the price of immortality, the toll extracted by the Void for the gift of eternity. He had learned to control it, to feed without killing, to take only what he needed. He had built discipline around it the way a mason builds walls around a fire: carefully, methodically, with the understanding that the fire was always there, always waiting, always hungry.

But discipline was a mortal virtue, and Vorenus was no longer mortal.

The hunger was not the worst of it.

He was afraid.

Not of the hunger — he had learned to control it, to feed without killing, to take only what he needed. He was afraid of what the hunger meant. The Thirst was the Void's mechanism for harvesting souls. Every time he fed, the energy flowed through him, into the Phantom Realm, to Nyktoros. He was a conduit, a channel, a weapon aimed at the world. The equation was simple and terrible: to survive, he had to feed. To feed, he had to feed the Void. To feed the Void was to feed Nyktoros. Every meal was a tithe. Every feeding was a surrender.

He had always been a weapon.

That was the truth that Vorenus carried like a stone in his chest, heavier than grief, colder than the blood that moved through his veins. He had always been a weapon — first in the mortal wars, where he had served the old kings of Eldoria with the discipline of a soldier and the ruthlessness of a man who believed in the cause. Then in the War of the Ancients, where Acheron had pointed him at the mortal armies and he had struck without hesitation. Then in the schism, where he had fought the fanatics in the deepest vaults, his blade singing through the dark, his silver eyes burning with the light of Auroria.

He had fought for Acheron, for the Covenant, for the chance to endure.

And now the Covenant was broken.

---

Before the Primal Pact, before the transformation, before the hunger and the silver light and the endless centuries, Vorenus had been a man.

Not a remarkable man. Not a king or a seer or a scholar. A soldier — a noble-born soldier of the old kingdoms, raised in the borderlands between Thaloria and the Emerald Fields, trained in the art of war by a father who had served three kings and died in the last of their wars. Vorenus had inherited his father's sword, his father's discipline, and his father's belief that duty was the highest virtue a man could possess.

He had been good at war. Not brilliant — brilliance belonged to the generals, the strategists, the men who moved pieces across a map. Vorenus was good at the thing itself: the clash of steel, the press of bodies, the moment when the world narrowed to a single point of focus and everything else fell away. He was fast, strong, precise. He fought with the cold efficiency of a man who had been trained to kill since boyhood, and he did not flinch from the work.

But he had always felt the emptiness.

There was a hollow space behind his ribs, a place where other men seemed to find purpose or meaning or the quiet satisfaction of a life well-lived. Vorenus could not find it. He fought, he won, he returned to his estates, and the hollow space remained. He served the kings faithfully, obeyed their commands without question, held the line when it mattered most. But the hollowness persisted, a quiet ache that no victory could fill.

He had been loyal then, too.

Loyalty was the one constant, the thread that ran through his mortal life and into his immortal one. He had been loyal to the kings, to the kingdom, to the soldiers who fought beside him. When Acheron came to him — silver-eyed, brilliant, offering the gift of eternity — Vorenus had recognized the same hunger that drove him: the need to serve something greater than himself, to be part of something that would endure beyond the span of a single mortal life.

He had accepted the Primal Pact without hesitation.

Acheron had warned him. The creator had stood before him, the Tome pulsing against his chest, and spoken of the Thirst, the hunger, the cost of the transformation. Vorenus had listened. He had understood. And he had accepted, because the hollowness demanded to be filled, and Acheron offered the only thing that could fill it: purpose.

The transformation had been agony. The fire of the Blood Ritual had torn through him, rewriting his flesh, rebuilding his body from the inside out. He had screamed — not in pain, though the pain was immense, but in something worse: recognition. He had recognized the hunger before he had understood what it was. It had been there, waiting, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, a presence as old as the Void itself.

And then Acheron had said: *You will endure.*

Vorenus had believed him.

He had believed because he had no choice — the hunger was already there, already burning, already demanding to be fed. He had to endure. There was nothing else.

In the early days, the hunger had been worse. Before the Soul-Leak, before the Vault's energy, before the golden age had given them sustenance and purpose, the Thirst had been a constant, screaming agony. Vorenus remembered the first century — the centuries of feeding, of hunting, of taking from the mortals what the Void demanded. He remembered the way the hunger had consumed him, had stripped away his discipline, had reduced him to something base and desperate. He remembered the killing — the first time he had taken too much, had drained a mortal to the edge of death, had felt the life force pour into him like water into a parched vessel.

He had felt the energy flow through him, into the Phantom Realm, to Nyktoros.

The realization had come later, years after the Blood Ritual, when the equations of the Tome had begun to reveal their true nature. Every feeding was a conduit. Every meal was a tithe. The energy did not simply sustain the vampire — it flowed through the vampire, through the conduits, through the connections that Acheron had carved into the stone. It went to Nyktoros. It fed the dormant intelligence. It opened the door.

And the door was opening wider with every pulse of the Soul-Leak.

Vorenus had not understood then. He had believed in the Covenant, in the Soul-Leak, in the chance to endure. He had believed that Acheron knew what he was doing, that the equations were balanced, that the patterns were set. He had been wrong.

Now, centuries later, standing in the ruins of the Sanguine Vaults, Vorenus wondered if the hollowness had ever truly been filled. The Covenant had given him purpose, the Soul-Leak had silenced the Thirst, the golden age had made him feel that his endurance mattered. But the Covenant was broken now, and the Thirst was back, and the golden age was over, and the hollowness was there, as vast and empty as it had always been.

He was a weapon without a war.

And the hunger was growing.

---

Vorenus had been loyal.

It was the defining fact of his existence, the axis around which his identity turned. Loyalty was not a choice for Vorenus — it was a compulsion, a gravitational pull that oriented his every action toward a single point: Acheron. He had been loyal when the fanatics rose, when Nyktoros's Herald led the charge into the deepest vaults, when the Phantom Realm bled through the cracks in reality and the air itself tasted of ozone and ash. He had fought with the discipline of a soldier, the fury of a believer, the desperation of a creature fighting for survival.

He remembered the battle. He remembered all of them — every clash of blade against Void-flesh, every scream that echoed through the tunnels, every moment when the world narrowed to a single point of focus: survive, endure, hold the line. He had held the line. He had always held the line.

But he had not held it alone.

Kael had been there.

The warrior had been the first of the first four — the strongest, the bravest, the most loyal. He had led the charge into the deepest vaults, his silver eyes burning with the light of Auroria, his body moving with the speed and strength that the Void had given him. Kael had been everything that Vorenus aspired to be: unwavering, absolute, certain. Where Vorenus questioned, Kael acted. Where Vorenus doubted, Kael believed. Where Vorenus hesitated, Kael struck.

They had been brothers.

Not in blood — Vorenus had long since forgotten what blood meant in the mortal sense. They had been brothers in the way that soldiers are brothers, in the way that creatures forged in the same fire are brothers, in the way that the first four were brothers: bound by the Primal Pact, by the transformation, by the Thirst that hummed in their bones and the silver light that burned in their eyes. They had trained together, fought together, fed together. They had stood side by side in the deepest vaults and watched Acheron study the Tome, had listened to the equations hum in the air, had felt the patterns vibrate at frequencies that made their blood sing.

They had believed.

And they had believed in each other.

Kael had been the best of them.

Vorenus remembered the training hall — a vast chamber in the outer Vaults, its walls carved with channels that hummed with the Void's energy, its floor laid with stone that resonated with the pulse of the Soul-Leak. They had trained there, the first four, their blades singing through the dark, their silver eyes gleaming with the light of Auroria. Kael had been the fastest, the strongest, the most precise. He moved like water — fluid, inevitable, unstoppable. When Kael fought, the world narrowed to a single point of beauty, and everything else fell away.

Vorenus had watched him train. He had stood at the edge of the hall, his blade resting at his side, and watched Kael move through the forms — the ancient Aurorian combat techniques that Acheron had adapted for vampire physiology. Kael's body was a weapon, honed by centuries of practice, sharpened by the energy of the Void. He struck with the force of a hurricane, retreated with the grace of a shadow, and when he was done, he would look at Vorenus and smile — a rare, quiet smile that said everything words could not.

"You're staring again," Kael had said once, after a training session that had left the stone floor cracked and the channels humming with excess energy.

"I'm learning," Vorenus had replied.

"You're staring," Kael repeated, but the smile was still there, warm and familiar, the smile of a brother who understood that loyalty did not need to be spoken to be felt.

They had stood together in the training hall, the energy of the Void humming around them, and Vorenus had felt the hollowness behind his ribs fill with something that was not quite peace but was close to it. The feeling of belonging. The feeling of being part of something greater than himself. The feeling of having found, at last, the purpose that had eluded him in his mortal life.

Kael had given him that.

Not Acheron — Acheron had given him the power to endure, the hunger, the silver light. But Kael had given him something more valuable: the knowledge that endurance was not loneliness. That the Thirst could be shared. That the endless centuries could be bearable, if they were spent in the company of someone who understood.

And now Kael was dead.

Vorenus had felt it — a connection severed, a bond broken, a presence that had been with him since the beginning. The fanatics had killed him in the deepest vaults, where the energy of the Void was strongest and the Phantom Realm bled through the cracks in reality like water through shattered stone. Kael had led the charge, his silver eyes blazing, his blade singing a song of Aurorian light against the darkness. He had cut through the fanatics like a scythe through wheat, his power absolute, his determination unshakeable.

And then Nyktoros's Herald had struck.

The blow came from behind, from the shadows, from the place where the Phantom Realm bled through most fiercely. A tendril of Void energy, black and writhing and alive with malice, had pierced Kael's chest. The warrior had staggered, his silver eyes wide with surprise — not with pain, not with fear, but with surprise. He had not expected to fall. He had never expected to fall.

Vorenus had watched it happen. He had been twenty paces away, fighting two fanatics at once, his blade singing through the dark. He had turned, just for a moment, and seen the tendril punch through Kael's chest, had seen the light drain from his silver eyes, had seen the warrior's body crumple to the stone floor like a puppet with its strings cut.

He had screamed.

The scream had come from somewhere deep and primal, from the place where the Primal Pact had rewritten his nature, from the place where loyalty was not a choice but a compulsion. He had fought his way to Kael's side, cutting through the fanatics with a ferocity that he had never known, his blade glowing with the light of Auroria, his silver eyes burning with grief and fury.

But it was too late.

Vorenus reached him. He dropped to his knees beside the warrior's body, his hands pressing against the wound in Kael's chest, trying to stem the flow of Void energy that pulsed from the injury like black light. The tendril had pierced clean through — a hole in Kael's chest the width of a man's fist, its edges wreathed in the dark, writhing energy of the Phantom Realm. The stone floor beneath Kael's body was blackened, scorched by the touch of the Void, and the air above the wound shimmered with a heat that was not heat.

Kael's eyes were open. The silver light that had burned in them for centuries was fading, dimming, receding like the tide. He looked at Vorenus, and his lips moved, and no sound came out. But Vorenus could read the words — he had known Kael long enough to read the silence between them.

*Hold the line.*

Kael died with those words on his lips, unspoken but understood. His body went slack, his silver eyes fixed on the vaulted ceiling, his hands still gripping the hilt of his blade. The Void energy that had sustained him for centuries dissipated, leaving behind flesh that was suddenly fragile, suddenly mortal, suddenly dead.

Vorenus held him.

He held the body of his brother, his comrade, his anchor in the chaos of war, and he felt the grief rise in his chest like a tide. It was not a quiet grief, not a dignified grief, not the measured sorrow of a soldier who has lost a comrade in the line of duty. It was a raw, primal thing, a howl that tore from his throat and echoed through the tunnels, reverberating off the stone, shaking the conduits and rattling the channels.

He screamed.

The scream had come from somewhere deep and primal, from the place where the Primal Pact had rewritten his nature, from the place where loyalty was not a choice but a compulsion. He had fought his way to Kael's side, cutting through the fanatics with a ferocity that he had never known, his blade glowing with the light of Auroria, his silver eyes burning with grief and fury.

Kael was dead.

Vorenus had not expected to feel grief. He was a soldier, trained to fight, to obey, to endure. He did not feel grief — he felt duty, loyalty, the quiet satisfaction of a battle won. Grief was a mortal emotion, a weakness of the flesh, a vulnerability that the Primal Pact was supposed to have burned away. But the Pact had not burned it away. It had only buried it, hidden it beneath layers of discipline and obedience and the cold, clean logic of survival.

And now Kael was dead, and the grief was there, and it was not quiet or clean or logical.

It was a howl.

A howl that echoed through the tunnels of the Sanguine Vaults, that reverberated off the stone, that shook the conduits and rattled the channels. It was the grief of a soldier who has lost his brother, his comrade, the one person who made the war bearable. It was the grief of a vampire who has lost the only other creature in the world who understood what it meant to carry the Thirst, to live with the hunger, to endure the endless, empty centuries.

Kael was gone.

The warrior, the loyalist, the first of the first four. Gone.

And the grief was a wound that would never heal.

---

The aftermath was silence.

The Sanguine Vaults were empty. The conduits were sealed. The pulse that had hummed in the walls for centuries was silent. The Soul-Leak was dead, and with it, the sustenance that had kept the Thirst at bay. The great city that Acheron had built — the subterranean metropolis, the marvel of the Covenant, the pride of the vampires — was a tomb. Its tunnels were dark, its chambers were cold, its walls were silent. The energy that had once filled every corner, that had hummed in the stone and pulsed through the conduits and made the Vaults glow with the light of the Void, was gone. Extinguished. Sealed. Dead.

The vampires scattered.

They left the vaults, left the estates, left the subterranean metropolis that Acheron had built. They moved through the tunnels like ghosts, their silver eyes dim with exhaustion, their bodies trembling with the hunger that the Soul-Leak had kept at bay. They broke into small cells, hid among the mortals, adopted the guise of humans. They were predators now, creatures of the night, living in the shadows of a world that did not know they existed.

Vorenus watched them go.

He stood in the central chamber and counted them as they left. Thirty-seven. Forty-one. Forty-six. The number dwindled with each passing hour, as vampires slipped away into the tunnels, their silver eyes fixed on the darkness beyond the Vaults. Some left in pairs, their bodies pressed together for comfort, their whispers lost in the vast silence of the stone. Some left alone, their faces masks of hunger and fear, their steps quick and purposeful, as if speed could outrun the Thirst. None of them looked at Vorenus. None of them spoke his name. They simply left, one by one, until the Vaults were empty and the tunnels were silent.

He was alone.

Acheron was gone — departed for the Black Well, for the ruins of Nythoria, to hide the Tome beyond the reach of the Void. The other vampires were scattered, hiding among the mortals, surviving on the blood of the living. They had left without looking back, without saying goodbye, without acknowledging the centuries they had spent together in the dark. They had simply left, one by one, their silver eyes empty, their faces masks of hunger and fear.

Vorenus did not blame them.

He understood. The hunger was a tyrant, and fear was its herald. When the Thirst returned in its full, terrible force, it consumed everything — thought, loyalty, identity. It reduced creatures of intelligence and power to something base and desperate, something that existed only to feed. The scattered vampires were not cowards or traitors. They were survivors. They were doing what they had to do to endure.

But endurance was not enough.

Not for Vorenus. Not for the lieutenant who had fought in the War of the Ancients, who had held the line in the deepest vaults, who had watched Kael fall and screamed his grief into the darkness. Endurance was not enough because endurance implied purpose, and Vorenus had no purpose. The Covenant was broken. Acheron was gone. The golden age was over.

Vorenus was a soldier without a war.

And the hunger was growing.

---

He walked through the empty tunnels, his silver eyes scanning the darkness. The Sanguine Vaults had been beautiful once — a city unto themselves, lit by the energy of the Void, humming with the equations of the Tome. Now they were ruins, silent and empty, their chambers filled with dust and their tunnels filled with shadows.

Vorenus had helped build this place.

He remembered the centuries of construction, the slow and patient work of carving the tunnels, building the chambers, designing the conduits. He had worked alongside Acheron, his silver eyes gleaming, his body sustained by the energy of the Void. He had believed in the Covenant, in the Soul-Leak, in the chance to endure. He had believed in Acheron — in his brilliance, his vision, his determination to give mortals the power to transcend.

He had been wrong.

The Covenant was broken. The Soul-Leak was dead. The chance to endure had become a curse, a trap, a weapon aimed at the world.

But the tunnels were still beautiful.

Vorenus ran his hand along the wall as he walked, feeling the stone beneath his fingers, the channels carved into the rock, the places where the energy had once flowed. The stone was cold now, dead, ordinary. But the channels were still there, etched into the rock with the precision of a scholar's hand. Acheron's hand. The First Vampire had carved these channels, had designed the flow of energy, had built a city that sustained hundreds of vampires for centuries.

And then he had abandoned it all.

Vorenus understood why. The Tome had to be hidden. The Soul-Leak had to be stopped. The connection to the Phantom Realm had to be sealed. Acheron had done what he had to do, what was necessary, what was right.

But understanding did not diminish the cost.

He passed through the outer chambers, where the lesser vampires had lived and fed and endured. The rooms were empty now, their furnishings stripped, their walls bare. The energy that had once filled them had receded, leaving behind stone that was cold and grey and lifeless. Vorenus could still feel it — the ghost of the Void's hum, the memory of the light, the echo of the sustenance that had kept the Thirst at bay.

He had fed in these chambers. He had drunk from the Soul-Leak, had felt the energy flow into him, had felt the Thirst subside. It had been easy then — a sip from an endless well, a meal from a source that never ran dry. The Soul-Leak had given them everything, and they had taken it for granted.

Now the well was dry.

And the hunger was growing.

He turned down a side corridor, one that led to the training hall — the vast chamber where the first four had honed their combat skills, where Kael had moved through the Aurorian forms with the fluid grace of water, where Vorenus had learned to fight not as a mortal but as something faster, stronger, more terrible. The hall was empty now, its stone floor cracked from centuries of impact, its walls scarred by the energy of countless training sessions. The channels that had once hummed with the Void's power were dark, their light extinguished, their vibration silenced.

Vorenus stood in the doorway and remembered.

He remembered the sound of Kael's blade — a high, clear singing that cut through the darkness like a bell through silence. He remembered the way Kael moved, the precise economy of motion, the devastating power hidden beneath a surface of calm. He remembered the way Kael would pause after a particularly difficult form, his silver eyes bright with the effort, and nod at Vorenus as if to say: *Now you try.*

Vorenus had tried. He had failed, many times, his blade clumsy and slow compared to Kael's elegant precision. But Kael had never mocked him, never grown impatient, never questioned his dedication. Kael had simply waited, his silver eyes patient, his hands clasped behind his back, and when Vorenus finally struck true — when the blade found its mark and the stone cracked beneath the force of the blow — Kael had smiled that rare, quiet smile and said: *Better.*

That was the thing Vorenus would miss. Not the battles, not the glory, not the power of the Void. He would miss the small moments — the training sessions, the quiet conversations, the shared silence of two soldiers who did not need words to understand each other.

He would miss Kael.

The grief was there, as it always was, a wound that would never heal. But it was quieter now, tempered by the passage of time and the weight of the Thirst. The grief was no longer a howl — it was a whisper, a constant, low-frequency ache that lived behind his ribs and whispered his name in the dark.

*You are alone,* the grief said. *You are alone, and you will always be alone.*

Vorenus closed his eyes and let the grief wash over him. It was familiar, this pain — as familiar as the hunger, as constant as the Thirst. He had learned to carry it, to hold it, to let it exist without letting it consume him. It was a part of what he was, a part of the Covenant, a part of the cost of loyalty.

And the cost was high.

---

He reached the central chamber.

It was the heart of the Sanguine Vaults, the place where Acheron had studied the Tome, where the equations had hummed in the air, where the patterns had vibrated at frequencies that mortal ears could not parse. The chamber was vast, its walls carved with channels that drew the energy from the Phantom Realm, its floor laid with stone that resonated with the Void. The ceiling was arched, designed to amplify the hum of the equations, to fill the space with the music of the Void.

The chamber was empty now. The Tome was gone. The equations were silent. The stone was cold.

But the hunger remained.

Vorenus stood in the center of the chamber and closed his eyes. He could feel it — the Thirst, gnawing at the edges of his mind, whispering in his blood, humming in his bones. It was a part of him, as fundamental as his silver eyes, as inescapable as the Void itself. He could suppress it, control it, direct it. But he could not escape it.

He opened his eyes and looked at the empty space where the Tome had been. He remembered the day Acheron had first brought it to the Vaults — the way the equations had hummed in the air, the way the patterns had shifted and changed with each reading, the way the light of the Void had filled the chamber with a luminescence that was not light. Acheron had been radiant that day, his silver eyes burning with the brilliance of Auroria, his hands trembling with the excitement of a scholar who has found the answer to the greatest question ever asked.

He had believed.

Vorenus had believed too. He had stood in this chamber and watched Acheron study the Tome, had watched the equations dance in the air, had watched the patterns vibrate at frequencies that made his blood sing. He had believed in the Covenant, in the Soul-Leak, in the chance to endure. He had believed that Acheron knew what he was doing, that the equations were balanced, that the patterns were set.

He had been wrong.

The Tome had been a trap. The Soul-Leak had been a weapon. The Covenant had been a harvest, and the vampires had been the crop, and Nyktoros had been the farmer, and the world had been the field.

And Acheron had known.

That was the thing that Vorenus could not forgive. Not the abandonment — he understood the necessity of that. Not the disbanding of the Covenant — he understood the logic of that. Not even the death of Kael — he understood the cruelty of war, the randomness of loss, the way that even the strongest and bravest could fall in a single moment.

What Vorenus could not forgive was the knowing.

Acheron had known what the Soul-Leak was doing. He had known where the energy was going. He had known that the Thirst was feeding Nyktoros, that the Covenant was sustaining the Void entity, that the door was opening wider with every pulse of the Soul-Leak. He had known, and he had continued, and he had let the vampires feed, and he had let the Thirst grow, and he had let the door open.

He had used them.

He had used them as bait, as lures, as fuel for a weapon that was aimed at the world. He had created the vampires not to endure, but to harvest. Not to transcend, but to feed. The Soul-Leak was not a gift — it was a funnel, a conduit, a channel that drew the life force of the vampires into the Phantom Realm and fed it to Nyktoros.

And Acheron had known.

Vorenus stood in the empty chamber and felt the grief rise in his chest. Not for Kael — that grief was a wound that would never heal, a scar that would never fade. But for himself, for the loyalty that he had given, for the belief that he had held, for the centuries of service that had been repaid with abandonment.

He had been a soldier.

He had been a good soldier.

And his commander had used him as a weapon.

---

He thought about Acheron.

The creator, the First Vampire, the one who had stolen the forbidden texts and defied the High Council. He had created the vampires, built the Covenant, carved the Soul-Leak into the stone. He had given them the power to endure.

And then he had abandoned them.

Vorenus stood in the central chamber and turned the thought over in his mind, examining it from every angle, the way Acheron had once examined the equations of the Tome. He understood the logic. He understood the necessity. The Tome had to be hidden. The Soul-Leak had to be stopped. The connection to the Phantom Realm had to be sealed. Acheron had done what he had to do, what was necessary, what was right.

But the cost was high.

The vampires were scattered. The Covenant was broken. The golden age was over. The Thirst was back, and the hunger was growing, and the Void was stirring in the depths.

And Kael was dead.

Vorenus had loved Kael.

Not in the way that mortals loved — not with warmth and softness and the fleeting bloom of youth. He had loved Kael in the way that soldiers love, in the way that creatures forged in the same fire love: with loyalty, with trust, with the absolute certainty that the other would hold the line when it mattered most. Kael had been his brother, his comrade, his anchor in the chaos of war.

And Acheron had let him die.

The thought was bitter, and Vorenus let it sit in his mind, examining it, turning it over. Had Acheron let Kael die? Or had Kael died because the war was cruel and the fanatics were strong and the Phantom Realm bled through the cracks in reality? Vorenus did not know. He did not want to know.
