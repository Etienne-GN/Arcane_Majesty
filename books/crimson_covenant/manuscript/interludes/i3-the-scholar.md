# Interlude III: The Scholar

The scholar heard the rumors in a tavern on the edge of Nythoria.

He was young — mortal young, perhaps twenty-five years of age, with silver eyes and a hunger for knowledge that burned in his veins like a second heartbeat. His name was not important. He was one of many, a researcher, a seeker, a mortal who had found fragments of the old texts and could not stop reading.

The rumors were about a book.

A book that contained the blueprints of Nyktoros, the energy schematics, the patterns that described how to siphon void energy into mortal flesh. A book that had been hidden in the depths of the Black Well, in the ruins of Nythoria, in the place where the Void was strongest.

The scholar did not believe the rumors.

But he could not stop thinking about them.

The tavern was called The Stone Crown, a name that suggested grandeur but delivered only decay. It sat at the edge of Nythoria like a half-forgotten tooth in the jaw of the world — a squat, timber-framed building with a thatched roof that sagged in the middle and walls that leaned at an angle that defied the laws of architecture. The air inside was thick with the smell of ale and sweat and the faint, sour tang of old wood, and the light was dim, filtering through grimy windows in dusty beams that illuminated the motes of dust that drifted through the stale air.

The scholar sat in a corner, his back to the wall, his silver eyes fixed on the rim of his cup. The ale was warm and bitter, but he barely tasted it. His mind was elsewhere — in the archives of the northern cities, in the fragments of parchment that he had spent years studying, in the equations that hummed in the old texts and vibrated at frequencies that mortal ears could not parse.

The tavern was quiet. A few mortals sat at the bar, their faces flushed with ale, their voices low and conspiratorial. The innkeeper — a stout man with a scarred face and hands that spoke of a life of labor — moved behind the bar with the slow, deliberate pace of a man who has learned that haste leads to mistakes. The air was thick with the smell of ale and sweat and the faint, sour tang of old wood, and the light was dim, filtering through grimy windows in dusty beams that illuminated the motes of dust that drifted through the stale air.

The scholar ignored them all. He was focused on the rumors, on the words that had reached him three days ago, carried on the lips of a merchant who had traveled from the coast. The merchant had not understood what he was saying — he was simply passing on a story, the way mortals always passed on stories, with embellishment, with speculation, with the casual disregard of someone who does not know what he is talking about.

But the scholar had understood.

He had understood because the words matched the fragments. They matched the equations, the patterns, the energy that pulsed in the old texts. They matched the blueprints, the energy schematics, the mechanisms that described how to siphon void energy into mortal flesh.

The book was real.

The scholar knew it the way he knew the equations — with the certainty of a mind that has been parsing data for years, that has found the patterns, that has seen the shape of the truth behind the noise.

The Forbidden Tome was real.

And it was in the Black Well.

The scholar's name was Aldric. He was twenty-five years old, born in a village on the banks of the Silverrun River, the son of a carpenter and a weaver. He had been a quiet child, introspective, more interested in books than in the games that other children played. He had learned to read at the age of four, had consumed every book in the village archive by the age of ten, had traveled to the city of Silverrun at the age of twelve to study in the Great Archive.

He had been different from the beginning. His eyes were silver — not the pale blue that passed for silver in the mortal world, but a true, metallic silver that caught the light and held it, that seemed to glow in the darkness. The villagers had whispered about it, had spoken of omens and portents, had told stories of the old bloodlines that had once ruled the world. Aldric had ignored them. He was a scholar, not a mystic, and he did not believe in omens or portents or the old bloodlines.

But the fragments had changed him.

He had found them in the archives, had felt the equations hum in his blood, had felt the resonance of the Void in the vellum. He had been transformed — not physically, not in the way that the old texts described, but intellectually, spiritually, in the way that a man is transformed when he discovers that the world is larger and stranger and more dangerous than he had ever imagined.

The fragments had given him purpose.

And the purpose had led him here, to a tavern on the edge of Nythoria, with a cup of warm ale in his hand and a hunger in his veins that he could not name.

The merchant had told the story in a tavern much like this one — a squat, timber-framed building with a thatched roof and walls that leaned at an angle that defied the laws of architecture. The air inside had been thick with the smell of ale and sweat and the faint, sour tang of old wood, and the light had been dim, filtering through grimy windows in dusty beams that illuminated the motes of dust that drifted through the stale air.

The merchant had spoken of a scholar — a young man with silver eyes and a hunger for knowledge that burned in his veins like a second heartbeat. The scholar had been seen in the ruins of Nythoria, had been heard asking questions about ancient dark lore, had been observed studying the equations that hummed in the old texts.

The name was Malakar.

The scholar had felt the blood drain from his face when he heard it. He knew the name. Not from the Tome, not from the equations, not from the blueprints of Nyktoros. He knew it from the Phantom Realm, from the energy that pulsed through the conduits, from the connection to the dormant intelligence that stirred in the depths.

Malakar was looking for the Tome.

The realization hit him like a physical blow. He stood in the tavern, his silver eyes fixed on the rim of his cup, and felt the equations of the Tome surge in his blood — a pulse of energy, a vibration, a resonance that echoed through the Phantom Realm like a bell struck in an empty room. The Tome was responding to the name, to the rumor, to the presence of someone who was seeking the knowledge that it contained.

The door was opening.

And Malakar was walking through it.

The scholar knew that he had to find the Tome before Malakar did. He had to reach the Black Well, had to descend into the depths, had to claim the power that slumbered in the darkness. The Void was calling to him, beckoning him, drawing him toward the Black Well. It wanted the Tome. It wanted the equations. It wanted the blueprints that described how to siphon void energy into mortal flesh.

And the scholar was going to give it to him.

The hunger was only beginning.

He had found the fragments in the archives of the northern cities — scraps of parchment, faded ink, the remnants of a civilization that had been old when the mortal world was young.

The first fragment had been an accident. He had been studying in the Great Archive of Silverrun, a city on the banks of the river that bore its name, cataloguing the mundane records of a long-dead merchant guild. The archive was vast, a labyrinth of shelves and corridors and reading rooms, and the scholar had been lost in it for weeks, surrounded by the dust of centuries and the silence of forgotten knowledge.

He had found the fragment in a crate of discarded papers — a sheet of vellum, yellowed with age, covered in symbols that he did not recognize. The symbols were not mortal. They were older, more complex, arranged in patterns that suggested equations rather than words. The scholar had stared at them for hours, trying to parse their meaning, trying to understand the language they spoke.

And then he had felt it.

A vibration, a hum, a resonance that seemed to emanate from the vellum itself. It was faint — so faint that he almost missed it — but it was there, pulsing in the air, vibrating in the stone, humming in the walls of the archive. The symbols were alive, animated by an energy that predated the mortal world, and the scholar had felt it in his blood, in his bones, in the hunger that had driven him to the archive in the first place.

He had stolen the fragment.

He had slipped it into his coat, had walked out of the archive, had not looked back. The theft was a small thing — a single sheet of vellum in an archive of millions, a missing page in a book that no one was reading. But the scholar had felt the weight of it, had felt the significance, had felt the equations humming in the vellum and vibrating in his blood.

He had found something.

He did not know what it was.

But he knew it was important.

The symbols were not mortal — they were older, more complex, arranged in patterns that suggested equations rather than words. They came at night, when the world was silent and the shadows were deep, and they called to him from the darkness, beckoned him, drew him toward the center of the ruins.

Toward the Black Well.

He took notes in his journal, analyzed the equations, parsed the patterns. He measured the energy flows, charted the frequency modulations, documented the resonance that hummed in the stone.

But he knew he had to find it.
